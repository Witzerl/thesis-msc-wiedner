\section{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:discussion:interpretation}

This thesis asked what is needed to forecast the progression of GA from
longitudinal OCT, split into a question about the framework and a question
about the operator (\S\ref{par:introduction:research-question}). The
results answer the two parts differently. The framework transferred: one
setup served operators from unrelated literatures without change. The
operator class did not decide the outcome; a small number of properties of
the operator did.

\paragraph{The framework.}
The framework fixes everything except the operator: the
$\Delta t$-conditioned residual update, the zero-initialised head that
starts every operator at persistence, the curriculum measured in elapsed
days, the loss, and one evaluation for all arms
(\S\ref{sec:method:framework}). Under these conditions, every operator
with any spatial context learned, and every one of them lies far above
persistence, which scores zero on the headline metric
(Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms}). This is what made the comparison of
Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} possible at all, and it is the answer to the
first part of the question.

How time enters the update turned out to matter less than that it enters.
A fourth-order Runge--Kutta step in place of one Euler step changed
nothing measurable, over a local and over a global operator
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:time}). The multiplication of the
operator output by $\Delta t$ is, on the mask channel, a change of
parameterisation rather than additional knowledge.

What the framework needs, in short, is that the length of the interval

\paragraph{The operator: ingredients, not classes.}
The three highest arms come from three unrelated classes --- a
finite-element network, a graph network and a U-Net --- and lie close
together (\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}). If the class decided the
outcome, such a result would be unlikely. What separates operators is
instead a set of properties that can be switched on and off one at a
time (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients}): spatial context at all,
physical reach at the scale of the lesion front, global and local context
together, and an explicit transport term.

The most informative of these is the pair of reach and transport. The GA
front moves about 10 to 25 columns between two visits, while one hop on
the index-space graph reaches about two columns along a row. Two unrelated
constructions close this gap: the dilated stencil, which lets one hop
reach 21 columns, and the transport term of the T-FEN, which moves the
state along a learned velocity. Each is established against its own
control, and the two end up indistinguishable from each other. Both point
to the same deficit: a mismatch between how far one update can see and
how far the disease moves between two observations. This is the
situation that the stability condition of \citet{Courant1928} describes
for explicit schemes, whose stencil must cover the region the solution
depends on within one step (\S\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers:time}).
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} stated this as an expectation for GA; the
survey is consistent with it. The coincidence of two routes is suggestive
rather than a proof of mechanism, but it is a more specific statement
than any ranking of architectures.

Global and local context behave as separable ingredients. The FNO, which
sees the whole grid in every layer but has no local path, lies below the
U-Net at the one-year anchor, and a $3 \times 3$ local path of 800
parameters brings it level (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:global}).
Beyond one year the plain FNO catches up with the U-Net and lies above it
after three years. These horizon values are descriptive and were not
tested, but they suggest that global support matters more for long
forecasts than for the one-year anchor.

\paragraph{Capacity.}
Capacity was not a lever in any of the four classes tested
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:capacity}). The dilated-stencil network
is flat from about a quarter to about four times its size, and the widest
U-Net, at about forty times its original parameter count, falls below the
original. With 75 eyes, this is what the low-data argument of
Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} leads one to expect: the assumptions built
into an operator weigh more than its size. The capacity study shows that
size did not help; that the built-in assumptions are the reason is an
interpretation, supported by the ingredient study.

\paragraph{A missing counterpart to the equation features.}
In MP-PDE, a vector of equation features $\theta_{\text{PDE}}$ --- the
coefficients and boundary conditions of the equation being solved --- is
passed to every layer, and it is what lets one trained solver handle a
whole family of equations \citep{Brandstetter2022}. GA has no governing
equation, and so no such vector exists. In this thesis the slot is filled
by $\Delta t$ and the two patient covariates (\S\ref{sec:method:covariates}).
The covariates are two numbers per visit, shared by every grid position
and nearly constant along a trajectory, so they can shift a prediction as
a whole but cannot say anything about a particular eye's lesion. The
learned stand-in that the code provides, a layer encoder that summarises
the retinal structure into one vector, was tested in an earlier
configuration and in one design only, without a detectable benefit.

What is lost is an eye-specific description of how this eye progresses.
Two eyes with the same lesion shape but different speeds receive the same
conditioning, and the operator can only infer the speed from the current
state. This is a plausible contributor to the growth-speed gap discussed
in \S\ref{sec:discussion:clinical}, but it was not tested. Candidates for
the slot are descriptors that do vary between eyes: lesion-level
quantities such as size, shape or focality, a learned summary of the
observed layers, or --- with trajectory memory --- the eye's own past
growth.

\paragraph{Predictions between visits.}
Two attempts were made to make the model meaningful between visits. An
intermediate-time-point regulariser, which penalised predictions at times
inside an interval against the linear interpolation between the two
visits, brought no benefit and was removed early in the project. The
network trained inside the Runge--Kutta wrapper, and the T-FEN in its
energy-conserving form, pass the solver-swap test and can therefore be
read as continuous-time models (\S\ref{sec:experiments:validity:swap}).
Neither is more accurate than the canonical network at the anchor, and the
wrapper costs about $4.8\times$ the training time. If predictions between
visits are needed clinically, these two are the principled choice. Their
predictions between visits remain unverified, however, because no ground
truth exists between two observations.

\paragraph{Where modelling effort pays.}
Taken together, the positive results and the nulls indicate where effort
paid off in this survey. Changing where an operator looks --- its reach
along the direction in which the front moves --- or adding a mechanism
that transports the state produced the largest effects. Larger models, a
higher integration order and the moving-mesh extension of
Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde}, whose correction branch never engaged, did not
improve accuracy at the available precision. An earlier ablation on an
older configuration of the graph network found the same for the settings
of its message function (normalisation, aggregation and direction
features). For a new disease-forecasting task of this kind, the first
question to ask of an operator is therefore how far one update reaches
compared with how far the disease moves between visits.


\section{Clinical Implications}
\label{sec:discussion:clinical}

\paragraph{Good at where, weaker at how fast.}
The survey was designed around the change-region Dice at one year, which
measures whether a model predicts change where change actually happens. On
this measure the operators are well above persistence. The speed of
growth of an individual eye over its whole follow-up is captured less
well. The correlation between predicted and true growth rates is 0.40 for
the canonical model, against 0.61 reported by \citet{Mai2024} on the same
cohort, and the fast-progressor AUCs are 0.70 to 0.74 against 0.77 to
0.81 (\S\ref{sec:experiments:qualitative:mai}). The other arms do not
differ from the canonical model on these measures, so the gap belongs to
the approach rather than to one operator.

The comparison is not like-for-like. \citet{Mai2024} start from the raw
OCT volume, whereas every model here starts from segmented masks and layer
depths; they evaluate 184 eyes with the full annotated lesion area,
whereas this thesis evaluates 75 eyes inside a crop that censors growth at
its border. It is still notable that a model given the segmented lesion
as input is weaker on growth speed than one that must find the lesion
itself.

Several explanations are possible, and none was tested. A local model
applied autoregressively over several years can drift, and the growth
rate is taken at the last visit, often three or more years after
baseline. Large, fast-growing lesions are the ones most often cut by the
crop, which lowers their measured growth. The operators see one visit and
have no memory of how fast the eye has grown so far. And, as argued in
\S\ref{sec:discussion:interpretation}, nothing in the conditioning tells
the operator how fast this particular eye progresses.

The distinction matters clinically. Complement inhibitors slow GA growth
but do not stop it (\S\ref{sec:introduction:motivation}), so the eyes
that grow fastest are the ones for which treatment is most worth its
burden. That decision depends on growth speed, which is the weaker of the
two capabilities. A forecast of where the lesion will expand is useful in
a different way, for example to judge whether growth is heading towards
the fovea, but that use was not evaluated here.

\paragraph{Reliable horizon.}
All comparisons in this thesis are made at the one-year anchor. Beyond
one year, the growth-region Dice of the canonical model does not fall; it
lies at about 0.60 to 0.66 in the second, third and later years
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:transport}). These values are
descriptive and pooled over rollout steps. Whether any of these levels is
clinically sufficient cannot be judged from the data: no clinically
established Dice threshold for GA progression forecasts is known to the
author.

\paragraph{Position in a clinical pipeline.}
Because every model starts from segmented masks and layer depths, a
clinical use would place it behind a segmentation model. Errors of that
segmentation would pass into the forecast, and this thesis does not model
them. Failures would matter most where the method is known to be weak:
lesions that touch the border of the crop, where growth is censored; fast
progressors; and eyes with sparse visit schedules, where each step must
bridge a long interval.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}

\paragraph{Data and labels.}
The cohort is small: 75 eyes of 51 patients, with 12 to 20 validation
eyes per fold. Every result is a validation result of a five-fold
cross-validation; there is no held-out test split
(\S\ref{sec:data:splits}). Twenty-four patients contribute both eyes, and
fellow eyes are not independent, so the per-eye statistics are somewhat
optimistic.

The fixed $49 \times 1024$ window is a deliberate trade-off with a
measured cost (\S\ref{sec:data:spatial}). It cuts real lesion area in
27.3\,\% of the visits, more than 5\,\% of the lesion in 6.0\,\%, and up
to 25.7\,\% in the worst case. Of the cropped lesions, 31.1\,\% touch the
crop border, so their growth across it is censored, in the training
targets and in the metrics, without any flag. Larger windows would trade
this for up to 35\,\% zero padding and about $1.9\times$ as many grid
positions, and about 19\,\% of lesions would touch the border anyway
because they reach the edge of the scan itself. A split of the headline
metric into border-touching and interior lesions was computed but is not
reported; the ranking of the arms does not change on the interior
lesions.

Every area in mm$^2$ rests on a pixel spacing that is treated as constant
across the cohort. It was checked only against the cohort's own
transform files, to about 2\,\%, and not against the original DICOM
spacing fields. The GA reference was most likely annotated on fundus
autofluorescence and registered onto the OCT grid, as described
by~\citet{Mai2024} for this cohort; the provenance of the layer
segmentation and the anatomical names of the ten boundaries are not
recorded. The reference masks also shrink locally between some visits.
Whether this reflects segmentation and registration noise or a real
change has not been quantified, and it is one reason why no
monotonic-growth constraint was imposed.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
The one-year anchor is the first rollout step at or beyond 360 days and
has no upper bound, so a few eyes are scored later, where more change has
accumulated. The change-region Dice was logged only as a cohort mean per
run, so the per-eye instrument uses the growth-region Dice instead
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}). Several findings rest on one
seed, and the solver-swap test on one fold per model.

\paragraph{Model scope.}
Every operator sees one visit and has no memory of the trajectory so far,
although each eye has 5 to 13 visits. An eye's own past growth, the most
direct evidence of its speed, is therefore never used. The patient
covariates are enabled in every operator, but whether they carry useful
information is not settled: an ablation on an earlier configuration found
no detectable benefit, and two numbers shared by every grid position
cannot localise anything. The pipeline starts from segmented masks, so
it neither benefits from nor accounts for the information in the raw OCT
volume.

The survey leaves out two classes that would complete it: a plain
recurrent network and a transformer. Physics-informed neural networks
were deliberately not run, because the governing equation is unknown and
the residual would have to be taken on a binary mask. The moving-mesh
null of Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde} is scoped to its implementation: the
gated correction branch did not engage, with or without the moving mesh,
which is not a statement about mesh adaptation for GA in general.

\paragraph{Unfinished replications.}
Several runs were still pending when this chapter was written: a second
seed of the T-FEN in its energy-conserving form, which decides whether its
lead over the U-Net is established; a second seed of the parameter-matched
transport control, which has so far been compared on four folds only;
the ablations of the $\Delta t$ multiplication and of the loss terms on the
canonical network; and the capacity points of the T-FEN in its
energy-conserving form.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion:future}

\paragraph{Spatial pre-processing.}
The fixed centre crop censors growth for about a third of the cropped
lesions. A larger or adaptive window with a mask for padded positions, or
a crop that follows the registration to the fundus image, would reduce
this censoring and make areas comparable with whole-field studies.

\paragraph{Trajectory memory.}
Giving the operator several past visits, or a recurrent state carried
along the rollout, would let it use the eye's own growth history. This
addresses the growth-speed gap of \S\ref{sec:discussion:clinical}
directly, and it would also bring in the two classes the survey is
missing, a recurrent network and a transformer.

\paragraph{An eye-level descriptor.}
A learned descriptor could take the place of the missing equation
features (\S\ref{sec:discussion:interpretation}). The one design tried
here saw predicted rather than observed layers during the rollout,
corrected only about $2\times$ of the grid's 21:1 anisotropy, and
conditioned the operator globally. A next design should see the observed
layers of the input visit, respect the physical spacing, and be able to
condition locally.

\paragraph{A valid transport model.}
The transport term is established, but the velocity field of the T-FEN
is not interpreted in this thesis. A velocity parameterisation that is
bounded by construction, starting from the energy-conserving form, would
give a model that stays numerically valid over multi-year horizons and
whose velocity field could be read as a direction and speed of growth.

\paragraph{From raw OCT, in 3-D.}
One way to address the gap to \citet{Mai2024} is to learn from the raw
OCT volume, either end to end or jointly with the segmentation. A further
step is to work on the volumetric OCT rather than on en-face maps.

\paragraph{Clinical use.}
A forecast used for treatment decisions needs a measure of its own
uncertainty, which none of the operators provides. Joint training across
several retinal diseases could also ease the data limitation.

\paragraph{The moving mesh.}
For the moving-mesh extension, the monitor that guides the mesh is
computed from the current state, whereas the change happens in the next
one. A monitor built on a surrogate of the next state, the open problem
named by \citet{Hu2024}, is the natural continuation
(Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde}).
