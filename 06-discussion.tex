\section{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:discussion:interpretation}
% 2026-10-19 rewritten (supervisor review 2, SV-6.1: self-contained for a
% reader who skipped the thesis; SV-6.2 micrometres; SV-6.5, SV-6.6; AI S14,
% S20, 6-7.1-6-7.12, 6-7.22-6-7.24). Run-in headings replaced by subsections;

This thesis asked what is needed to forecast the progression of GA from
longitudinal OCT. The question had two parts: what kind of framework the
forecast needs, and what kind of operator that framework needs
(\S\ref{par:introduction:research-question}). The task was to predict,
from the lesion mask and ten retinal layer boundaries of one visit, the
lesion at the later visits of the same eye. The intervals between visits
range from 90 to 720 days and are most often 180 days. The data were 75
eyes of 51 patients from the Medical University of Vienna, evaluated in
five-fold cross-validation. Every model was scored by the change-region
Dice at one year: the overlap between the pixels the model predicts to
change and the pixels that do change. Predicting no change, persistence,
scores 0 by construction. This section interprets the results for each
part of the question and then draws the implications for modelling.

\subsection{The Framework}
\label{sec:discussion:framework}

The framework is everything that stays fixed while the operator changes
(\S\ref{sec:method:framework}). It predicts the next visit from the
current one by a residual update,
$u_{t+\Delta t} = u_t + \Delta t \cdot f_\theta(u_t, \Delta t, z)$, whose
operator $f_\theta$ starts at zero, so that every model begins at
persistence. It trains with a loss that weights the mask channel five
times and adds a soft-Dice term, and it unrolls its own predictions over up
to 360 days during training. Inside this framework, every operator with
spatial context learned to predict change: the eleven such arms reach a
change-region Dice of 0.45 to 0.54 at one year
(Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms}), while the per-pixel floor, which sees
no neighbours, stays at 0. One framework thus served operators from
unrelated literatures without change. This is the answer to the first
part of the question.

Its parts do not matter equally. The objective matters most. Trained with
a plain mean squared error, without the weighting of the mask channel and
without the soft-Dice term, the canonical network learned no change at
all: at the last epoch, 74 of the 75 eyes are predicted exactly as at
baseline (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:loss}). With the weighting but
without the soft-Dice term, it learned change, but later and to a score
lower by 0.050. A penalty against lesion shrinkage added nothing. The time
handling, in contrast, can be simplified without loss. Removing the
multiplication by $\Delta t$ left the result unchanged, because the
operator learns the division by the interval internally. Replacing the
single Euler step by four Runge--Kutta evaluations made no measurable
difference for either a graph network or a Fourier Neural Operator
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:time}). The update needs the length of
the interval, but not in a particular form. These tests were run on the
canonical network, or on two operators for the integration scheme, so
they describe the framework as it is used here, not in general.

\subsection{The Operator}
\label{sec:discussion:operator}

Within the framework, the three highest arms come from three unrelated
classes: the T-FEN, a finite-element network with a transport term, at
0.543; the dilated-stencil graph network at 0.526; and a U-Net at 0.505
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}). The T-FEN lies above the U-Net at
both seeds, and its lead of 0.017 over the graph network is not
established. The class of an operator therefore does not decide its
score. The ablations of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients} tested three
properties one at a time on otherwise unchanged operators:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Spatial context is required: a graph network that sees only each
    pixel itself predicts no change.
  \item The reach of one update along the direction in which the lesion
    front moves is the largest lever. Replacing the index-space
    neighbourhood of the graph network by the dilated stencil raises its
    score by 0.064, at the same parameter count.
  \item An explicit transport term raises the finite-element network by
    0.062 against the same network without it, and by 0.072 against a
    control of the same size.
\end{itemize}
A local path for the global FNO brought it level with the U-Net at one
seed but not at the second, and its own effect is not established.

Reach and transport are the most informative pair. The GA front advances
about 57 to 142\,\textmu{}m between two visits, 10 to 25 columns of the
grid, while one hop of the index-space graph reaches about 11\,\textmu{}m
along a row (\S\ref{sec:method:graph}). The dilated stencil lets one hop
reach about 119\,\textmu{}m, and the transport term moves the state along
a learned velocity. These two unrelated constructions each remove the same
limit, and the two arms built with them are the two highest of the survey.
The limit is the one that the stability condition of~\citet{Courant1928}
describes for explicit schemes: the stencil of one step must cover the
region on which the solution depends
(\S\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers:time}).
Section~\ref{sec:introduction:why-pde} stated this as an expectation for
GA, and the survey is consistent with it. The agreement of the two
constructions is suggestive but does not prove a mechanism. It is
nevertheless a more specific statement than a ranking of architectures.
% 10-25 columns x 5.68 um = 57-142 um; +/-2 columns = 11.4 um; +/-21
% columns = 119 um (SV-6.2).

More reach does not keep helping. Reaches of $\pm 35$ and $\pm 42$ columns
score lower than $\pm 21$, the latter beyond the noise level. Widening the
stencil across rows lowers the score most. Four message-passing rounds
instead of two, which double the reach, lower the canonical network by
0.009 (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:reach},
\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:capacity}). The best tested reach, about
119\,\textmu{}m, lies close to the 90th percentile of the advance between
visits. Why reach beyond that point hurts was not tested. With mean
aggregation, far neighbours may dilute the near ones, and the extra reach
may bring in tissue unrelated to the next visit's change.

Global context behaves differently. The FNO sees the whole grid in every
layer but lies below the U-Net at one year. Beyond one year it catches up,
and after three years it lies above the U-Net
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:global}). These horizon values are
descriptive and were not tested statistically, but they suggest that
global context matters more for long forecasts than at one year.

\subsection{Capacity}
\label{sec:discussion:capacity}

Capacity was not a lever in any of the four classes tested
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:capacity}): no width step raised a class
above the noise level, and the smaller versions lost little. With 75 eyes, this is what the low-data argument of
\S\ref{sec:introduction:why-pde} leads one to expect: the assumptions built
into an operator weigh more than its size. The capacity study shows that
size did not help; that the built-in assumptions are the reason is an
interpretation, supported by the ablation studies.

\subsection{A Missing Counterpart to the Equation Features}
\label{sec:discussion:equation-features}

In MP-PDE, a vector of equation features $\theta_{\text{PDE}}$ --- the
coefficients and boundary conditions of the equation being solved --- is
passed to every layer, and it is what lets one trained solver handle a
whole family of equations \citep{Brandstetter2022}. GA has no governing
equation, and so no such vector exists. In this thesis the slot is filled
by $\Delta t$ and the two patient covariates (\S\ref{sec:method:covariates}).
The covariates are two numbers per visit, shared by every grid position
and nearly constant along a trajectory, so they can shift a prediction as
a whole but cannot say anything about a particular eye's lesion. The
learned stand-in that the code provides, a layer encoder that summarises
the retinal structure into one vector, was tested in an earlier
configuration and in one design only, without a detectable benefit.

What is lost is an eye-specific description of how this eye progresses.
Two eyes with the same lesion shape but different speeds receive the same
conditioning, and the operator can only infer the speed from the current
state. This is a plausible contributor to the weaker prediction of growth
speed discussed in~\S\ref{sec:discussion:clinical}, but it was not tested.
Candidates for the slot are descriptors that do vary between eyes:
lesion-level quantities such as size, shape or focality, a learned summary
of the observed layers, or --- with trajectory memory --- the eye's own
past growth.

\subsection{Predictions Between Visits}
\label{sec:discussion:between-visits}

Every operator of the survey is trained to predict the state at the next
visit, so its predictions between visits are not constrained by the data.
Two models nevertheless define a prediction at any time. The T-FEN in its
energy-conserving form passes the solver-swap test on all three tested
folds and can therefore be read as a continuous-time model
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:validity:swap}). The network trained inside the
Runge--Kutta wrapper also defines a vector field, but its training step,
one RK4 step per interval, resolves that field on only one of the three
folds. Neither is more accurate than the canonical network at one year,
and the wrapper costs about $4.8\times$ the training time. If predictions
between visits are needed clinically, these two models give predictions
between visits that are consistent with those at the visits; the wrapper
network would need a finer training step. Whether these predictions are
correct cannot be checked, because no ground truth exists between two
observations. An earlier attempt to constrain them, a regulariser that
penalised predictions between visits against the linear interpolation of
the two visits, brought no benefit and was removed before the experiments
of Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}.

\subsection{Implications for Modelling}
\label{sec:discussion:implications}

Across the survey, the largest effects came from two changes to the
operator: extending the reach of one update along the direction in which
the front moves, and adding a mechanism that transports the state. Larger
models, a higher integration order, a penalty against lesion shrinkage and
the moving-mesh extension of Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde}, whose correction
branch never engaged, did not improve accuracy at the available precision.
In the framework, the objective decided whether the model learned any
change at all. For a new disease-forecasting task of this kind, two
questions therefore come first: whether the loss puts enough weight on the
region that changes, and how far one update of the operator reaches
compared with how far the disease moves between visits.


\section{Clinical Implications}
\label{sec:discussion:clinical}

The survey was designed around the change-region Dice at one year, which
measures whether a model predicts change where change actually happens. On
this measure the operators are well above persistence. The speed of
growth of an individual eye over its whole follow-up is captured less
well. The correlation between predicted and true growth rates is 0.40 for
the canonical model, against 0.52 to 0.61 for the comparable models
of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:qualitative}, and its fast-progressor AUCs are
0.70 to 0.74 against 0.77 to 0.81 reported by \citet{Mai2024} on the full
cohort from which the data here are drawn. The other operators of the
survey give similar values, so the weaker agreement on growth speed is
shared by every operator in the framework.

The comparison is not like-for-like
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:qualitative:mai}). It is still notable that a model given the segmented lesion
as input is weaker on growth speed than one that must find the lesion
itself.

Several explanations are possible; none of them was tested:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A local model applied autoregressively over several years can
    drift, and the growth rate is taken at the last visit, often three or
    more years after baseline.
  \item Large, fast-growing lesions are the ones most often cut by the
    crop, which lowers their measured growth.
  \item The operators see one visit and have no memory of how fast the eye
    has grown so far.
  \item The conditioning carries no information on how fast this
    particular eye progresses (\S\ref{sec:discussion:equation-features}).
  \item The operators are not trained on the growth rate. It is computed
    afterwards from their predicted maps, whereas some models
    of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:qualitative} regress the rate directly.
  \item The operators predict less change than occurs in most eyes, 35 of
    48 on folds 0 to 2 at a median of 0.73 of the true change
    (Figure~\ref{fig:experiments:rollout}), and about half the true rate
    for the fastest eyes (\S\ref{sec:experiments:qualitative}). A
    deterministic model trained with a pixel-wise loss may favour such
    cautious predictions.
  \item The inputs carry no image intensity. The models
    of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:qualitative} with stronger growth-speed
    values use reflectivity maps or the raw OCT volume, and for fundus
    autofluorescence images the tissue around the lesion carries the most
    information on growth speed (\S\ref{sec:background:related-work}).
    Masks and layer depths may hold less of that information.
\end{itemize}

The distinction is clinically relevant. Complement inhibitors slow GA
growth but do not stop it (\S\ref{sec:introduction:motivation}), so the
benefit of treatment relative to its burden is largest in the eyes that
grow fastest. The decision to treat therefore depends on growth speed, the
weaker of the two capabilities. A forecast of where the lesion will expand is useful in
a different way, for example to judge whether growth is heading towards
the fovea, but that use was not evaluated here.

A second clinical question is how far ahead the forecast remains useful.
All comparisons in this thesis are made at the one-year anchor, the
first visit about one year after baseline. Beyond one year, the
growth-region Dice of the canonical model, which scores only new atrophy,
does not fall; it
lies at about 0.60 to 0.66 in the second, third and later years
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:transport}). These values are
descriptive and pooled over rollout steps. Whether any of these levels is
clinically sufficient cannot be judged from the data: no clinically
established Dice threshold for GA progression forecasts is known to the
author.

A scale for these values comes from the reference outlines themselves.
When two trained graders outline the same fundus autofluorescence images,
their outlines of the region that became atrophic within one year agree
with a Dice of only about 0.71 to 0.73 \citep{Salvi2025}. The outlines
that serve as ground truth therefore carry considerable uncertainty of
their own, and a Dice of 1 is not reachable in practice. On this scale,
the first-year growth-region Dice of about 0.5 obtained here
(Table~\ref{tab:experiments:mai-bins}) lies closer to the attainable level
than its distance from 1 suggests. The grader value is a reference point,
neither a target nor an upper limit for the models of this thesis. It was
measured on a different imaging modality and a different cohort, and the
graders outlined existing images rather than predicting a future one.

% forecasts found. Salvi et al. 2025 (PMC11699103), Results / Table 6:
% inter-grader DSC for the 1-year region of growth 0.71 +/- 0.12 (training),
% 0.72 +/- 0.14 (validation), 0.73 +/- 0.10 (test); their best model 0.70 +/-
% 0.09. Whole-lesion inter-grader values (0.88-0.94) measure something else.

Finally, because every model starts from segmented masks and layer
depths, a clinical use would place it behind a segmentation model. Errors of that
segmentation would pass into the forecast, and this thesis does not model
them. Forecast failures are most likely where the method is known to be
weak:
lesions that touch the border of the crop, where growth is censored
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:folds}); fast progressors, whose growth is
under-predicted (\S\ref{sec:experiments:qualitative}); and eyes with
sparse visit schedules, where each step must bridge a long interval.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}

The cohort is small: 75 eyes of 51 patients, with 12 to 20 validation
eyes per fold. Every result is a validation result of a five-fold
cross-validation; there is no held-out test split
(\S\ref{sec:data:splits}). Twenty-four patients contribute both eyes, and
fellow eyes are not independent, so the per-eye statistics are somewhat
optimistic.

The fixed $49 \times 1024$ window censors growth at its border, in the
training targets and in the metrics, without any flag
(\S\ref{sec:data:spatial}). On the 54 eyes whose
lesion does not touch the crop border, the arms keep the order of
Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms} (\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}).

Every area in mm$^2$ rests on a pixel spacing that is treated as constant
across the cohort. It was checked only against the cohort's own
transform files, to about 2\,\%, and not against the original DICOM
spacing fields. The GA reference was annotated on fundus
autofluorescence and registered onto the OCT grid (\S\ref{sec:data:dataset}).

The reference masks also shrink between visits. The total
lesion area decreases in 5\,\% of the 478 pairs of consecutive visits, by a
median of 2.6\,\%, but almost every pair (96\,\%) loses some atrophic
pixels along the border, a median of 2.2\,\% of the lesion. This pattern is
more consistent with annotation and registration noise at the boundary
than with regression of the atrophy, although the masks alone cannot
separate the two. The observed shrinkage is one reason why no
monotonic-growth constraint was imposed.

The evaluation has limits of its own. The one-year anchor is the first
visit at or after about one year, and its time
has no upper bound, so a few eyes are scored later, where more change has
accumulated. The change-region Dice was logged only as a cohort mean per
run, so the eye counts use the growth-region Dice instead
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}). Several findings rest on one
seed, and the solver-swap test on three of the five folds per model.

The scope of the models is limited as well. Every operator sees one visit
and has no memory of the trajectory so far,
although each eye has 5 to 13 visits. An eye's own past growth, the most
direct evidence of its speed, is therefore never used. The patient
covariates are enabled in every operator, but whether they carry useful
information is not settled; two numbers shared by every grid position
cannot localise anything. The pipeline starts from segmented masks, so
it neither benefits from nor accounts for the information in the raw OCT
volume.

Two directions lie outside the survey. Physics-informed neural networks
were deliberately not run, because the governing equation is unknown and
the equation residual would have to be taken on a binary mask. The second
is mesh adaptation: all operators work on the uniform $49 \times 1024$
grid. Adapting the mesh
to the lesion, so that grid points concentrate where the front moves, might
help, but it excludes the operators that need a regular grid, such as the
U-Net and the FNO. A moving-mesh extension of the graph network was tested
as one additional experiment (Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde}); no conclusion
about mesh adaptation is drawn from it.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion:future}

\paragraph{Spatial pre-processing.}
The fixed centre crop censors growth at the window border
(\S\ref{sec:data:spatial}). A larger or adaptive window with a mask for padded positions, or
a crop that follows the registration to the fundus image, would reduce
this censoring and make areas comparable with whole-field studies.

\paragraph{A continuous representation of the lesion.}
% Author review v6 #9 (2026-10-02): "future: the continuous distance from
% lesion experiment".
The lesion enters the state as a binary mask, so the normalised state has
a hard step at the lesion edge (\S\ref{sec:data:normalization}), and a
pixel that becomes atrophic has to jump the full distance between the two
mask values in a single update (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:time}).
A signed distance to the lesion boundary would replace the step by a
smooth field whose zero level marks the boundary. Growth would then appear
as a gradual change of this field rather than as jumps at single pixels,
and the binary forecast would be recovered by thresholding at zero.
Whether this makes the forecast easier to learn, including the growth
speed that the binary state predicts less well
(\S\ref{sec:discussion:clinical}), is untested.

\paragraph{Trajectory memory.}
Giving the operator several past visits, or a recurrent state carried
along the rollout, would let it use the eye's own growth history. This
would address the growth-speed gap of \S\ref{sec:discussion:clinical}
directly. It is also the setting in which sequence models such as
recurrent networks and transformers become applicable.

\paragraph{An eye-level descriptor.}
A learned descriptor could take the place of the missing equation
features (\S\ref{sec:discussion:interpretation}). The one design tried
here saw predicted rather than observed layers during the rollout,
corrected only about $2\times$ of the grid's 21:1 anisotropy, and
conditioned the operator globally. A next design should see the observed
layers of the input visit, respect the physical spacing, and be able to
condition locally.

\paragraph{A valid transport model.}
The transport term is established, but the velocity field of the T-FEN
is not interpreted in this thesis. A next step is a velocity
parameterisation that is bounded by construction and builds on the
energy-conserving form. A model with such a parameterisation would stay
numerically valid over multi-year horizons, and its velocity field could
be read as a direction and speed of growth.

\paragraph{From raw OCT, in 3-D.}
One way to address the gap to \citet{Mai2024} is to learn from the raw
OCT volume, either end to end or jointly with the segmentation. A further
step is to work on the volumetric OCT rather than on en-face maps.

\paragraph{Clinical use.}
A forecast used for treatment decisions needs a measure of its own
uncertainty, which none of the operators provides. Joint training across
several retinal diseases could also ease the data limitation.
