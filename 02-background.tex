\chapter{Background}

This chapter provides the background on which the later chapters build.
Section~\ref{sec:background:ga-oct} introduces the anatomy of the eye,
the methods used to image the retina and the disease itself.
Section~\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers} introduces partial differential
equations and the numerical methods that solve them, and
Section~\ref{sec:background:neural-pde} the neural PDE solvers, which
replace parts of such a solver, or the whole update, with a neural

two strands: it explains why the structure of a numerical solver suits
the forecasting of GA and states the problem formally.

Figure~\ref{fig:bg:eye-anatomy} (left) shows the eye in cross-section.
Light enters at the front through
the \emph{cornea} and \emph{lens}, traverses the gel-filled vitreous
body, and is focused onto the \emph{retina} --- a thin
neural-tissue lining at the back of the eye that converts the
incoming light pattern into electrical signals. The conversion takes
place in the \emph{photoreceptors}, the light-sensitive cells of the
retina. Those signals leave
the eye via the \emph{optic nerve}, which exits the retina at a
specific anatomical landmark called the \emph{optic-nerve head} (ONH,
also called the \emph{optic disc}). Behind the retina lie two further
layers that support and supply it from beneath: the \emph{choroid}, a
richly vascularised tissue whose innermost capillary bed is the
\emph{choriocapillaris}, and the \emph{sclera}, the white, mechanically
rigid outer shell. The inside of the back of the eye, as seen through
the pupil, is called the \emph{fundus}.

The retina is not uniform across its surface. A small disc-shaped
region, the \emph{macula}, is specialised for
high-acuity central vision, while the rest of the retina handles
peripheral vision at lower resolution. The macula has a diameter of
roughly five to six millimetres and is the only part of the retina
that GA affects clinically. The OCT scanning window used throughout
the thesis is therefore centred on the macula and covers a $6 \times 6$\,mm field
(\S\ref{sec:background:ga-oct:muw}). Within the macula, four
concentric anatomical zones are conventionally distinguished, in
order of increasing radius from the centre: the \emph{foveola}, the
\emph{fovea}, the \emph{parafovea}, and the \emph{perifovea}
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg:eye-anatomy}, right). The fovea is the small
central pit at which photoreceptor density and visual acuity reach
their peak, and the term ``foveal'' refers to anything in or close to
that pit; ``parafoveal'' and ``perifoveal'' describe progressively
more eccentric annular regions. Two qualifiers used in the GA
literature follow directly from this geography: a lesion is called
\emph{subfoveal} if it overlaps the fovea (i.e.\ it directly threatens
high-acuity vision) and \emph{extrafoveal} otherwise. As discussed
in~\S\ref{sec:introduction:motivation}, whether a lesion is subfoveal or
extrafoveal changes its clinical significance even if its
total area is the same.

The retina is also a \emph{layered} tissue, with several distinct
strata stacked vertically from the front (closest to the vitreous
body) to the back (closest to the choroid). The principal layers, in
order from inner to outer, are the inner limiting membrane, the
retinal nerve fibre layer, the ganglion-cell and inner-plexiform
layers, the inner nuclear and outer plexiform layers, the outer
nuclear layer, the photoreceptor inner- and outer-segment bands, the
retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and Bruch's membrane;
Figure~\ref{fig:bg:retinal-anatomy}(a) gives a labelled schematic.
For the purposes of this thesis, it suffices to note that the retina is
layered, that GA destroys a specific \emph{outer}-retinal stack of three
layers (\S\ref{sec:background:ga-oct:disease}), and that the surrounding
layer geometry, given as ten boundary depth maps, is passed to the model
together with the lesion mask (\S\ref{sec:background:ga-oct:state-rep}).

The remaining subsections build on this vocabulary.
\S\ref{sec:background:ga-oct:oct} outlines the technical side of retinal
imaging, from the acquisition of an OCT volume to the 2D en-face maps
that the model operates on. \S\ref{sec:background:ga-oct:disease}
describes how GA appears in these OCT images. From this, the structure
of the data follows: a binary lesion mask together with the geometry of
the surrounding retinal layers, which will be further elaborated in
Chapter~\ref{ch:data}.

\label{sec:background:ga-oct:oct}

Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), introduced by \citet{Huang1991},
produces micrometre-resolution cross-sectional images of the retina.
The principle is analogous to ultrasound imaging of soft tissue, with
low-coherence near-infrared light in place of sound. A weak probe beam is
directed into the eye, and the strength of the light reflected back from
each \emph{depth} along its path is measured. This depth-versus-reflectivity
profile is called an \emph{A-scan}. It is displayed as a single column of
pixels, with intensity encoding reflectivity and vertical position
encoding depth. Resolving the depth dimension requires an optical
technique, because conventional electronics cannot time near-infrared
light from a single short pulse precisely enough to separate individual
layers. The standard solution is interferometry. The source light is
split into two beams (Figure~\ref{fig:bg:oct-acquisition}(a)): the
\emph{sample arm} is sent into the eye and reflected back from the
various retinal layers, and the \emph{reference arm} is sent to a known
mirror and reflected back unchanged. The two returning beams are then recombined and produce an
interference pattern, and the spectrum of that pattern (in
spectral-domain OCT) encodes the depth at which each reflection in
the eye occurred. Standard signal processing then recovers the
A-scan as a one-dimensional function of depth.

A single A-scan is one column of one image. Scanning the probe beam
laterally along one direction produces many adjacent A-scans, and
displaying them side by side yields a two-dimensional cross-section
through the retina, called a \emph{B-scan}
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg:oct-acquisition}(b)). Scanning in both lateral
directions yields a three-dimensional \emph{OCT volume}
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg:oct-acquisition}(b)), in which each voxel records
the reflectivity of one small block of tissue. The MUW cohort uses an
acquisition protocol that produces volumes with a lateral footprint of
approximately $6 \times 6$\,mm. How such a volume is summarised as a
two-dimensional en-face image is described below, and how the varying
native grids are brought to one modelling grid in~\S\ref{sec:data:spatial}.

Two further imaging modalities are used alongside OCT. Scanning laser
ophthalmoscopy (SLO) scans a laser point by point across the fundus and
records the reflected light, giving a sharp two-dimensional image of the
retinal surface.

The OCT device used for the cohort records a near-infrared reflectance
image of this kind together with each volume, aligned with the OCT scans
\citep{Mai2024}. Fundus autofluorescence (FAF) imaging records the
natural fluorescence of lipofuscin, a breakdown product of photoreceptor
outer segments that accumulates in the RPE. Where the RPE is lost, the
signal is lost, so GA appears as a dark area with high-contrast borders
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg:ga-faf}),
and FAF has become the gold standard for detecting and measuring GA area
\citep{Boopathiraj2024}.

OCT is preferred over fundus autofluorescence (FAF), the other common
imaging modality for GA, for three reasons given by \citet{Vallino2024}: it
shows each retinal layer in cross-section and so permits layer-wise
quantitative analysis, it detects atrophy earlier in its natural history,
and it remains reliable near the fovea, where blue-light FAF is less reliable.
OCT-based area measurements are also reproducible and consistent with FAF:
\citet{Pilotto2015} report inter-reader intraclass correlation coefficients
of 0.97--0.98 for en-face OCT at the outer-retinal level and find the
resulting GA areas at that level statistically equivalent to blue-light and
near-infrared FAF measurements in a small cohort. The thesis therefore works on the OCT en-face grid.

A single OCT volume shows both the lesion and the retinal layers around it.
The lesion itself is
visible on the B-scan as a region of outer-retinal layer dropout with
choroidal hypertransmission; the surrounding retinal layer geometry is
recoverable from the same volume by segmentation; and the
choroidal slab beneath Bruch's membrane provides an additional view of
the GA extent through the hypertransmission signature
\citep{Vallino2024,Chu2022}.


\paragraph{From OCT volumes to en-face maps.}
\label{sec:background:ga-oct:enface}

The full OCT volume of a GA eye is too large and too redundant to be
modelled at native voxel resolution. The clinical literature works with an \emph{en-face} image instead: a two-dimensional map of the
retina seen from the front, as in a fundus photograph, in which every pixel
corresponds to one A-scan of the volume and summarises what lies along it
\citep{Pilotto2015,Chu2022,Yehoshua2011,Vallino2024}
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg:oct-acquisition}(b), right). The present work
adopts the same convention.

Several ways of forming the en-face image from the volume are used in the
literature, for example summing the full depth of each A-scan
\citep{Yehoshua2011} or taking only a thin coronal section at a fixed depth
relative to Bruch's membrane \citep{Pilotto2015}. No projection is computed in this thesis. Instead, the MUW
data provide two kinds of en-face maps for every visit, on the same grid:
the GA lesion as a binary mask, and, for each of ten retinal layer
boundaries, its depth along every A-scan (\S\ref{sec:data:dataset}). These
maps are the imaging input the models in this thesis receive.

The en-face representation is also the view on which quantitative
growth-rate metrics and layer-thickness analyses are reported in the
recent literature \citep{Chu2022,Vogl2021,Vallino2024}. The en-face grid
also reduces the forward problem of each step from three dimensions to
two, which matters for the tractability of the models in the following
chapters.

Figure~\ref{fig:bg:retinal-anatomy}(a) sketches the principal
anatomical strata of the retina in the macula. Geographic atrophy is the
disease in which three tightly coupled \emph{outer}-retinal layers die
together in a localised patch of the macula: the retinal pigment
epithelium (RPE), the photoreceptor outer segments just above it, and the
choriocapillaris, a dense capillary bed immediately below it
\citep{Boopathiraj2024,Boyer2017}. These three layers are
anatomically interdependent: the RPE supports the photoreceptors, and
the choriocapillaris supplies both. Atrophy of one layer therefore leads
to the degeneration of the others, and the resulting lesion appears on imaging as a sharply delineated patch
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg:ga-faf}) in which the entire
outer-retinal complex is missing rather than as the loss of a single
isolated layer.

An international consensus group, the Classification of Atrophy
Meetings (CAM), has formalised the OCT appearance of this end-stage
lesion under the term \emph{complete RPE and outer retinal atrophy}
(cRORA) \citep{Sadda2018, Vallino2024}. cRORA is identified by three concurrent
OCT features: \emph{(a)} a region of choroidal hypertransmission of at
least 250\,\textmu{}m in diameter; \emph{(b)} co-located attenuation or
disruption of the RPE band of at least 250\,\textmu{}m; and \emph{(c)}
overlying photoreceptor degeneration, all in the absence of an RPE tear.
cRORA is thus the OCT-readable definition of an established
atrophic lesion, which can arise with or without macular neovascularisation
(the wet form of AMD), and \emph{GA is the subset of cRORA that
arises in eyes without macular neovascularisation}. The CAM framework
also recognises a precursor stage, \emph{iRORA} (incomplete RPE and
outer retinal atrophy), which shows the same OCT pattern below the
250\,\textmu{}m size threshold. In one retrospective study of
intermediate-AMD eyes, 93\,\% of iRORA lesions progressed to cRORA within
twenty-four months, with a median conversion time of fourteen months
\citep{Vallino2024}. Working on en-face maps rather than on single
B-scans is also supported by a caveat documented by \citet{Vallino2024}:
a lesion classified as iRORA on a single horizontal B-scan can be part of
a larger cRORA lesion that is fully visible only in the en-face slab.
Channel~0 of
the state tensor used in this thesis marks the GA lesion as a binary
mask (\S\ref{sec:background:ga-oct:state-rep}); iRORA is not represented
as a separate label.

An established GA lesion is identified on a B-scan by three principal
direct OCT signatures \citep{Boopathiraj2024,Yehoshua2011,Vallino2024}.
\emph{(i)} The outer-retinal layers are missing: the photoreceptor
inner-segment / outer-segment junction (the \emph{ellipsoid zone}) and
the RPE band drop out of the B-scan, leaving a structurally collapsed
column where intact retina used to be.
\emph{(ii)} \emph{Choroidal hypertransmission} appears beneath the
lesion: because pigmented RPE no longer absorbs the probe beam, light
penetrates deeper into the choroid, and the column-summed signal is
higher than in the surrounding intact tissue. This is the same optical
effect that makes GA appear bright on summed-voxel en-face projections,
discussed in~\S\ref{sec:background:ga-oct:enface}.
\emph{(iii)} \emph{RPE--Bruch's-membrane (BM) thickening} is
concentrated in a narrow rim just outside the lesion margin and is
thought to reflect, at
least in part, basal-laminar deposits accumulated in the diseased tissue;
the thickness of this rim correlates significantly with the eye's subsequent
annual square-root lesion enlargement rate \citep{Chu2022}.
The first two signatures delineate the lesion itself. The third is a
predictive biomarker located at the lesion boundary and motivates
encoding the surrounding layer geometry in addition to the lesion mask.


\section{Partial Differential Equations and Numerical Solvers}
\label{sec:background:pde-solvers}
The models in this thesis are built like numerical solvers for partial
differential equations (PDEs): a state is held on a grid and advanced
step by step by an update rule. The later
chapters therefore use the vocabulary of numerical solvers throughout.
The update of the framework is one explicit Euler step in residual form
(Chapter~\ref{ch:method}). The operators placed in it are learned
stencils, global spectral methods and finite elements. The experiments
ask how far one update reaches, whether a higher-order time integrator
helps, and when a step becomes unstable (Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}).
This section introduces these mechanisms.

A \emph{temporal} PDE describes a field $u(t, x)$ that changes over time
$t$ at every point $x$ of a spatial domain $\mathcal{X} \subset
\mathbb{R}^D$. It states how fast the field changes at each point, given
the current field around that point:
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u \;=\; F\!\left(t, x, u, \partial_x u, \partial_{xx} u, \dots\right),
  \qquad (t, x) \in [0, T] \times \mathcal{X},
  \label{eq:bg:temporal-pde}
\end{equation}
where $u: [0, T] \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ has $n$ components
and $F$ is the law of the process. Because $F$ involves only $u$ and
its derivatives at $x$, and derivatives are local quantities, the rate
of change at a point depends only on the field in an arbitrarily small
neighbourhood of it. The stencils of
\S\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers:stencils} exploit this locality. Together with an
initial condition $u(0, x) = u^{0}(x)$ and a condition on the domain
boundary, \eqref{eq:bg:temporal-pde} determines $u(t, \cdot)$ for all
$t > 0$. Closed-form solutions exist only in special cases, so the
solution is computed numerically. A numerical solver does this in two
steps: it replaces the continuous domain by a grid and the spatial
derivatives by operations on grid values
(\S\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers:stencils}), and it then advances the
grid values in discrete time steps
(\S\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers:time}).
\S\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers:limits} then summarises the strengths
and limits of this design.

\subsection{Discretising space: stencils}
\label{sec:background:pde-solvers:stencils}

The domain is partitioned into a grid of $N$ small cells $c_i$ with
centres $x_i$, and the solution is stored as one value per cell and
time step, $u_i^{k} \approx u(t_k, x_i)$;\newline
$u^{k} = (u_1^{k}, \dots, u_N^{k})$ denotes the whole grid. The simplest
grid is a uniform one, a regular array of equal rectangular cells with
constant spacings $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$. The two spacings
need not be equal. On such an anisotropic grid, a stencil that is
symmetric in grid indices reaches different physical distances along the
two axes.

\paragraph{Finite differences and finite volumes.}
The finite difference method (FDM) replaces each spatial derivative by
a difference of neighbouring grid values \citep{LeVeque2007}. On a
uniform one-dimensional grid, the central approximation of the first derivative is
\begin{equation}
  \left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right|_{x_i}
  \;\approx\; \frac{u_{i+1} - u_{i-1}}{2 \Delta x}.
  \label{eq:bg:fdm-central}
\end{equation}
In general, the $n$-th derivative at cell $i$ is estimated as a
weighted sum over a small neighbourhood $\mathcal{N}(i)$,
\begin{equation}
  \left[\partial_x^{(n)} u\right]_i
  \;\approx\; \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \alpha_j^{(n)} u_j ,
  \label{eq:bg:stencil}
\end{equation}
with fixed coefficients $\alpha_j^{(n)}$ obtained by fitting a local
polynomial to the neighbourhood and differentiating it. The
neighbourhood together with its coefficients is called a
\emph{stencil}. Inserting such stencils into $F$ gives a function $f$
that maps the grid values $u^{k}$ to a rate of change for every cell.
The finite volume method (FVM) follows the same pattern, but applies
its stencils to the flow of a quantity between neighbouring cells
rather than to derivatives at cell centres \citep{LeVeque2002}.

\paragraph{Finite elements.}
The finite element method (FEM) divides the domain into simple
elements, typically triangles, and represents the solution as a sum of
basis functions, each of which is non-zero only on the elements around
one node \citep{Courant1943, LangtangenMardal2019}. With
piecewise-linear basis functions, the coefficients are exactly the node
values. Instead of requiring \eqref{eq:bg:temporal-pde} to hold at every
point, the method requires it to hold on average against each basis
function, the so-called \emph{weak form}. Because each basis function
overlaps only those of its immediate neighbours, every node is coupled
only to the nodes it shares an element with. The result is again a
local stencil, but one defined by the mesh rather than by a regular
grid, which makes the method well suited to irregular domains. The
Finite Element Networks of \S\ref{sec:background:neural-pde} learn their
dynamics inside this discretisation.

\paragraph{Adaptive stencils.}
A fixed stencil that straddles a sharp front produces spurious
oscillations next to it. Weighted essentially non-oscillatory (WENO)
schemes avoid this by computing several candidate estimates on shifted
sub-stencils. The estimates are combined with weights that depend on how
smooth the data are in each sub-stencil, so that sub-stencils crossing
the front receive almost no weight \citep{Liu1994, JiangShu1996}. The
stencil thereby becomes a non-linear function of its input, which makes
WENO the classical mechanism closest to what a learned operator does.

\paragraph{Global methods.}
Pseudospectral methods compute derivatives in Fourier space instead of from a neighbourhood: the
field is transformed, each mode is multiplied by $(i\omega)^n$, and the
result is transformed back. The derivative at every point then depends
on all grid values. The accuracy is excellent for smooth solutions on
periodic domains, but the method breaks down for non-smooth solutions
\citep{GottliebOrszag1977, Trefethen2000}.

\paragraph{Locality and reach.}
Stencil methods are \emph{local}: the rate of change of a cell is
computed from a small neighbourhood, so one evaluation of $f$ moves
information by at most one stencil radius. This distance, the
\emph{reach} of the stencil, is set in physical units by the width of the
stencil and by the grid spacing. The
contrast between local stencils and global methods reappears in the
neural architectures of~\S\ref{sec:background:neural-pde}.

\subsection{Stepping in time}
\label{sec:background:pde-solvers:time}

The stencils of \S\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers:stencils} reduce the
spatial part of the problem to the rate function $f$; the grid then has
to be advanced in time with this rate.
The two parts are independent: the same $f$ can be combined with any
time-stepping scheme, and the choice of scheme does not change how $f$
is computed.
The simplest time-stepping scheme is the explicit Euler step \citep{Butcher1987, Hairer1993},
\begin{equation}
  u_i^{k + 1} \;=\; u_i^{k}
  \;+\; \Delta t \, f_i\!\left(t_k, u^{k}\right),
  \qquad \Delta t = t_{k+1} - t_k ,
  \label{eq:bg:euler}
\end{equation}
which holds the rate constant over the step. Runge--Kutta methods reach
higher accuracy by evaluating $f$ several times within a step and
combining the results; the classical fourth-order scheme (RK4) uses four
evaluations.

Spatial stencil and time integrator together define an update operator
\begin{equation}
  u^{k+1} \;=\; \mathcal{A}\!\left(\Delta t, u^{k}\right),
  \label{eq:bg:update-operator}
\end{equation}
and a trajectory is produced by applying $\mathcal{A}$ repeatedly from
$u^{0}$, each step consuming the output of the previous one. This
repeated application is called a \emph{rollout}.
Figure~\ref{fig:bg:solver-path} summarises the path.

Two properties decide whether such an update is usable. The first is
\emph{consistency}: as the step shrinks to zero, the update must
reproduce the exact solution,
$\lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \lVert \mathcal{A}(\Delta t, u^{0}) - u(\Delta t) \rVert = 0$
\citep{Brandstetter2022}. An update written as the current state plus
$\Delta t$ times a rate, as in~\eqref{eq:bg:euler}, is said to be in
\emph{residual} form; it returns the current state at $\Delta t = 0$
whatever the rate. The second is \emph{stability}: errors made in one
step must not grow from step to step. For explicit schemes this bounds
the step size.
The Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL) condition \citep{Courant1928}
states that, for a process that moves information at speed $c$, one
step may not carry information further than the stencil reaches,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{c \, \Delta t}{\Delta x} \;\leq\; C_{\max},
  \label{eq:bg:cfl}
\end{equation}
with a constant $C_{\max}$ of order one that depends on the scheme.
Equivalently, a stencil used with a given step must be wide
enough to cover the distance the solution moves during that step.

\subsection{Strengths and limits}
\label{sec:background:pde-solvers:limits}

Classical solvers are well understood. Each component has a known error
behaviour, convergence to the true solution can be proven for broad
classes of problems as the grid is refined \citep{LeVeque2002}, and no
training data are needed.

Their limits follow from the same design. The equation $F$, including its
coefficients, must be known, because the solver can only compute what
$F$ prescribes. Good schemes are hand-crafted for each
class of equation. Following \citet{Bartels2016}, \citet{Brandstetter2022}
describe the field of numerical methods as a ``splitter field'' that
builds a dedicated solver for each sub-problem, rather than a ``lumper
field'' that seeks one method for all of them. Accuracy is also expensive. Fine
features need fine grids, the CFL condition then forces small time steps,
and the cost grows faster than the resolution.


\section{Neural PDE Solvers and Surrogate Architectures}
\label{sec:background:neural-pde}
% Restructure 2026-10-12: the former 2.2.4 "Neural solvers that keep the
% mechanisms" opens this section (learned solvers introduced once, AI
% review T8/S17).

\label{sec:background:pde-solvers:neural}

Every mechanism of~\S\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers} is a function from a neighbourhood of grid values
to a number: the stencil coefficients, the WENO weights, the rate that
the time step applies. Any of them can be learned from data instead of
derived from an equation, and much of the neural-solver literature does
exactly that while keeping the rest of the computation. \emph{Hybrid}
solvers keep the classical scheme and learn selected parts of it.
\citet{BarSinai2019} learn the stencil coefficients
of~\eqref{eq:bg:stencil} and obtain accurate solutions on considerably
coarser grids than hand-designed stencils allow; \citet{Kochkov2021}
apply the same idea inside a solver for turbulent flow.
\emph{End-to-end} solvers learn the whole update $\mathcal{A}$ but keep
its shape: a state on a grid or mesh, a local operator, a step in time.
Graph neural networks built this way simulate fluids, deformable
materials and cloth \citep{SanchezGonzalez2020, Pfaff2021}.
A graph-network weather forecaster trained on decades of
reanalysis data outperforms a leading operational numerical model on
most of its verification targets \citep{Lam2023}.

The message-passing solver of \citet{Brandstetter2022} (MP-PDE) makes
the correspondence between message passing and a classical stencil
explicit. The grid becomes a graph whose nodes are
the cells and whose edges connect each cell to its stencil
neighbourhood. One round of message passing computes, at every node, a
learned function of its neighbours' values and of their differences to
its own value; it thus acts as a learned, non-linear stencil
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg:solver-path}, panel~4). The authors show that one, two and three rounds can
represent finite differences, finite volumes and WENO respectively, so
the classical schemes are special cases of the learned one. The output
is applied in the residual form of~\eqref{eq:bg:euler}, which keeps the
update consistent. The graph operator of this thesis is built on this
solver and is described in~\S\ref{sec:method:mppde}.

A learned solver does not require $f$ to be written down. Because $f$
is fitted to data, the solver can be used where the equation is
expensive to solve, known only approximately, or unknown, and it can
often run on coarser grids with larger steps. In exchange, the classical
guarantees are lost. A learned solver needs training data, has no
convergence proof, and in a rollout it consumes its own predictions, so
its errors can accumulate from step to step. This problem and its
remedies are discussed later in this section.

Learned solvers also differ in what they predict, and in this respect
they separate into two paradigms. Neural
operators learn a continuous map
$M: [0, T] \times \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{F}$ such that
$M(t, u_0) = u(t)$, and so predict the solution at any time directly
from the initial condition. By contrast, autoregressive
solvers learn the update operator $\mathcal{A}$ of
\eqref{eq:bg:update-operator} and apply it repeatedly to unroll a
trajectory. When the step size $\Delta t$ is fixed by the dataset, the
dependence on it is usually omitted from the notation. Here it cannot be
omitted, because the intervals between clinical visits are irregular.

Finite-dimensional neural operators are bound to the grid geometry they
were trained on, whereas infinite-dimensional ones are defined
independently of the spatial discretisation. The Fourier Neural Operator
\citep{Li2021} parameterises a kernel integral operator in the Fourier
domain, which gives the network global spatial support within a single
layer. DeepONet \citep{Lu2021} instead factorises the operator into a
branch network that encodes the input function and a trunk network that
encodes the continuous query coordinate. Both share a well-known
limitation: a trained operator is tied to the equation family it was
trained on, and its extrapolation beyond the temporal horizon seen during
training is consistently poor.

The autoregressive framing is the natural fit for clinical
longitudinal data. Each visit-to-visit transition constitutes one step. Since there is no
shared temporal origin ($t = 0$) across patients, a direct
time-dependent mapping is ill-posed. In addition, the prediction horizon
is set by the clinic rather than by the model, so the model has to
support as many steps as a patient's visit schedule requires.

The principal cost of this framing is the error accumulation noted at
the start of this section: after the first step, the solver consumes its
own output instead of numerical ground truth, so the inputs it receives
during a rollout differ systematically from those seen in training. This
distribution shift is the central training
difficulty for autoregressive rollouts.

Together with their message-passing solver, introduced above,
\citet{Brandstetter2022} propose two mechanisms against this distribution
shift.
The first is temporal bundling: the model predicts $K$ future steps in
one forward pass. A rollout of a given length then needs $K$ times fewer
solver calls, and the model is exposed to fewer shifts in its input
distribution. The second is the pushforward trick. During training, the
input state is replaced by the model's own one-step prediction from the
preceding state, so the model is trained on the kind of input it will
meet during a rollout. Two steps are unrolled, and gradients are
propagated only through the last. Backpropagating through the first step
as well would teach the model to make its errors small rather than to
correct them.

The authors relate the pushforward trick to zero stability. An update
operator is zero-stable if a small perturbation of its input changes the
output only by a bounded multiple,
$\lVert \mathcal{A}(u^0 + \epsilon) - u^1 \rVert \leq \kappa \lVert
\epsilon \rVert$, and the pushforward loss reduces the constant
$\kappa$. Together with the consistency of the residual form
(\S\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers:time}), zero stability is the
classical basis of a convergence argument. Chapter~\ref{ch:method}
describes how the framework of this thesis treats temporal bundling and
the pushforward trick.

Beyond graph networks, several distinct architecture families are used
as PDE surrogates.
The U-Net \citep{Ronneberger2015} is an encoder-decoder
architecture with skip connections and a multi-scale receptive field.
It serves as a standard strong surrogate baseline in the neural-PDE
literature \citep{Gupta2023}.
The architectures also differ in their spatial context: global
spectral operators such as the Fourier Neural Operator couple all grid
points within one layer, whereas convolutional and message-passing
operators are local. Hybrid architectures add a small local kernel
bypass to a global operator
to capture high-frequency details more effectively
\citep{LiuSchiaffini2024}.
Finite Element Networks \citep{Lienen2022} build the neural update
directly from a finite-element discretisation with learned dynamics, and
their transport variant carries an explicit transport (advection) term.
Finally, because a residual step resembles one explicit Euler step, the
network is sometimes read as the right-hand side of an ordinary
differential equation, the Neural-ODE reading \citep{Chen2018}. A
higher-order integrator such as a Runge--Kutta scheme can then in
principle replace the residual step. This reading is only legitimate if
the trained model is nearly invariant to the solver used at inference.
The property has to be tested empirically, because classical solver
guarantees do not automatically transfer to neural parameterisations
\citep{Ott2021, Krishnapriyan2023}.

The architecture families differ mainly in four structural properties:
whether the operator has spatial context at all, how far that context
physically reaches, whether it combines global and local information,
and whether it carries an explicit transport term. Such built-in
assumptions of a model are called its inductive biases.
Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} measures the four properties one at a time.

Work related to this thesis falls into four groups: models that predict
the risk or the rate of AMD and GA progression, models that forecast
where a GA lesion will grow, models that forecast disease progression
from irregularly sampled longitudinal data in general, and PDE-based
models in biomedicine. The original papers of the architectures used as
the operator are cited where each is introduced, in

\subsection{Predicting the Risk and Rate of Progression}
\label{sec:background:related-work:rate}

Learning-based models of AMD and GA progression on OCT fall broadly into
models that predict a risk or a rate for each eye and models that
predict where the lesion will grow.
\citet{SchmidtErfurth2018} reported on the prediction of individual
disease conversion in intermediate AMD using artificial intelligence.
\citet{Bogunovic2017} used machine learning to predict the regression of
individual drusen, a sign of disease activity, in intermediate AMD from OCT
imaging. \citet{Vogl2021} provided a topographic analysis of the retinal
and choroidal layer changes that precede the conversion of intermediate
AMD to macular atrophy or neovascularisation. \citet{Vallino2024} noted that
artificial intelligence applied to OCT has the potential to provide
predictive probabilities of GA development over time.

Deep-learning models have also been trained to predict the growth rate
of GA in an individual eye. From OCT volumes, four different approaches
reached a squared correlation of 0.33 to 0.35 between predicted and
observed growth rate on a holdout set \citep{Yoshida2025}. For growth-rate
models trained on FAF images, occlusion experiments showed that a rim of
tissue around the lesion contributes more to the prediction than the
lesion itself or the background \citep{Cluceru2024}.

\subsection{Spatial Forecasting of GA Lesions}
\label{sec:background:related-work:spatial}

Closer to the task of this thesis are models that forecast the lesion
itself. \citet{Gigon2021} predicted the progression of RORA from OCT
en-face maps of layer thicknesses, mean reflectivity and drusen height in
129 eyes of 119 patients. Their model gives each en-face pixel a
probability of atrophy over time, from which a map of the time to
conversion follows; the Dice score ranged from 0.73 to 0.80 for the total
RORA area and from 0.46 to 0.72 for the growth region.
\citet{Lachinov2024} encode each pixel of a baseline scan as a feature
vector and evolve these vectors with a Neural ODE \citep{Chen2018} to any
requested time point, using a fourth-order Runge--Kutta scheme. Their
model was developed on 967 OCT volumes of 100 patients with GA and also
applied to brain MRI in Alzheimer's disease. The Runge--Kutta-integrated
operators of this thesis (\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}) are related to
this continuous-time formulation.

% Cohort relation confirmed by the supervisor (review 1, 2026-10-12): same
% cohort, with tighter registration bounds and cleaner segmentations.
The closest prior work on the specific prediction task addressed here
is by \citet{Mai2024}, who predicted individual GA progression from a
single baseline OCT on the MUW GA cohort (184 eyes of 100 patients). The
data of this thesis are drawn from the same cohort, with tighter
registration bounds and cleaner segmentations (75 eyes of 51 patients,
\S\ref{sec:data:dataset}). The input regime, however, is different. That
work operates directly on raw OCT volumes, whereas the present thesis
uses pre-segmented lesion masks and layer surfaces.
As a precedent for dense convolutional architectures on this task,
\citet{Salvi2025} predicted future GA growth with a two-dimensional
U-Net on fundus autofluorescence images.

On a finer scale, the border of a GA lesion advances at different local
speeds. The lateral spread of GA was slowest at regular borders, at about
63\,\textmu{}m per year, faster at irregular borders, at about
91\,\textmu{}m per year, and fastest at splitting borders, at about
183\,\textmu{}m per year \citep{Lindner2018}.

Most of the clinical literature formulates prediction at cohort level,
as a categorical time-to-conversion problem. The formulation used here
instead requires a per-eye, per-time-step prediction of the full spatial
next state.

\subsection{Forecasting from Irregularly Sampled Longitudinal Data}
\label{sec:background:related-work:irregular}

Irregular intervals between observations are a general property of
longitudinal clinical data. Latent ODE and ODE-RNN models let a hidden
state evolve in continuous time between observations and so handle
arbitrary time gaps \citep{Rubanova2019}. For images, ImageFlowNet learns
a multiscale latent representation and a flow field in it with a neural
ODE or SDE, and forecasts image-level trajectories of disease
progression. It was evaluated on FAF images of GA, on multiple sclerosis
and on glioblastoma, an aggressive brain tumour
\citep{Liu2025ImageFlowNet}. In brain imaging,
4D-DANI-Net generates subject-specific longitudinal MRI scans that mimic
neurodegeneration in ageing and dementia \citep{Ravi2022}, and BrLP
combines a latent diffusion model with an auxiliary model of regional
volume changes to predict the evolution of individual 3-D brain MRIs
\citep{Puglisi2024}. These models treat time continuously or condition
on it; the operators of this thesis take the interval as an input to a
single residual step (\S\ref{sec:background:approach}).

\subsection{PDE-Based Models in Biomedicine}
\label{sec:background:related-work:pde}

Where the governing equation of a disease process is known, PDE models
are fitted to individual patients. For glioblastoma, tumour growth
models based on the Fisher--Kolmogorov equation are personalised with
Bayesian inference from MRI and FET-PET scans to design radiotherapy
plans \citep{Lipkova2019}. Learned components speed this up: a neural
surrogate that replaces the numerical solver of such a tumour model is
about 50 times faster \citep{Ezhov2022}, and a network that solves the
inverse problem infers the patient-specific tumour cell distribution
within minutes \citep{Ezhov2023}. Physics-informed machine learning in
medical image analysis more broadly has been reviewed in a survey of
over 80 papers \citep{Banerjee2025}. In all of these, the equation is
known and the network learns to solve or invert it. Learned neural PDE
solvers trained directly on longitudinal clinical images without a known
equation have, to the author's knowledge, not been applied to GA and are
rare in medical imaging.

\section{Modelling Approach and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:background:approach}

This section brings the clinical and the numerical background together.
It first explains why the structure of a numerical solver suits the
forecasting of GA (\S\ref{sec:introduction:why-pde}) and then states the
problem formally, in the notation used in the remaining chapters
(\S\ref{sec:background:problem}).


\subsection{Choice of the Modelling Approach}
\label{sec:introduction:why-pde}
\label{sec:background:pde-solvers:ga}
% Restructure 2026-10-12 (supervisor review 1): collected from the former
% 1.2 Problem Statement (data properties, framework), 1.3 Why Neural PDE
% Solvers? (local growth, anisotropy, neural solvers) and 2.2.5 Why this
% structure suits GA progression; bridged 2026-10-13 (author sign-off).

Clinical OCT follow-up gives the data three properties that a model must
handle. First, follow-up visits are
\emph{irregularly spaced} in time and the spacing varies substantially
between patients and between consecutive visits of the same patient.
Irregular spacing rules out methods that assume a fixed sampling rate, and
the lack of a shared temporal origin across eyes rules out methods that
assume a common $t = 0$. Second, the disease state is
\emph{multi-channel}: a binary mask marks the GA lesion, and ten retinal
layer boundaries describe the retinal structure in which the atrophy lies.
The layer channels are relevant because prognostic biomarkers
documented in the clinical literature, such as RPE--Bruch's-membrane
thickening \citep{Chu2022} and thinning of the outer retinal layers
\citep{Vallino2024}, are not contained in the lesion mask alone. Third, every patient carries \emph{covariates}, in particular age and
sex. Age is strongly associated with the risk of developing the disease
(\S\ref{sec:introduction:motivation}), and the evidence on sex is mixed;
whether either also helps to forecast where and how fast a lesion grows is
not established.


Handling these properties depends on the model and on how it is used. A typical image-to-image model maps one image
to another; on its own, it has no notion of elapsed time and no built-in way
to advance a state from one visit to the next. Both abilities have to be
supplied by a framework around the model that conditions the prediction
on $\Delta t$ and advances the state from one visit to the next. Such a framework
can borrow several of its techniques from numerical and neural PDE
solvers, as the remainder of this subsection explains. Which model should
sit inside it is a separate question.


GA expansion is, to a first approximation, a process of \emph{local boundary
dynamics}. \citet{Yehoshua2011} found that the enlargement rate of the square
root of the lesion area does not depend on the baseline lesion size. This is
what is expected if the radius of a GA lesion grows at a roughly constant
rate while its enclosed area scales quadratically. With $A(t)$ the lesion
area and $r(t)$ an effective radius,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}t} \approx \mathrm{const.}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \sqrt{A(t)/\pi} \;=\; r_0 + v\, t,
  \label{eq:intro:boundary-growth}
\end{equation}
which is consistent with the data within a clinical follow-up horizon of six
months to slightly over two years \citep{Yehoshua2011}; the square-root
enlargement rate has been re-used in subsequent biomarker work
\citep{Chu2022}. The interior of the lesion is already atrophic and the
exterior is healthy; both are quasi-static, and all dynamics are
concentrated at the active boundary. Such behaviour is characteristic of
a system governed by a temporal PDE with strong spatial localisation.


The same boundary, however, does not advance uniformly in all directions.
GA growth is \emph{anisotropic}: lesions progress faster towards the retinal
periphery than towards the fovea, and growth towards the fovea peaks at
approximately one millimetre from the fovea \citep{Singh2025}. In eyes
treated monthly with pegcetacoplan, progression towards the fovea was
slowed more than progression towards the periphery \citep{Singh2025}. The
forward operator that maps the present state to the future state is
therefore \emph{local}, but its reach must match the distance the front
moves between two visits, and this distance differs between directions.
Classical solvers build such properties in explicitly through the size and
shape of their stencils.


GA progression has no known governing equation, and its data are a few
observed states per eye (\S\ref{sec:background:ga-oct:muw}) instead of a
densely simulated trajectory. A classical solver therefore cannot be used.
The structure such a solver is built from, however, matches the problem in
four respects, and the thesis adopts it for these reasons.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A state on a fixed grid.} Every visit is represented on
    the same uniform en-face grid (\S\ref{sec:background:ga-oct:state-rep}),
    so the state is exactly the vector of grid values $u^{k}$ that a
    solver advances.
  \item \textbf{Change driven by the neighbourhood.} As described above,
    GA grows at the margin of the lesion, while its atrophic interior and
    the surrounding retina change little (Figure~\ref{fig:bg:ga-faf}). Whether a pixel becomes atrophic
    depends on what surrounds it, which is the situation a stencil is
    designed for.
  \item \textbf{Slow change.} Consecutive visits differ in a small part
    of the grid. The residual form predicts only the change and returns
    the current state as $\Delta t \to 0$; its default, the unchanged
    state, suits data that change slowly.
  \item \textbf{Irregular steps.} The intervals between visits differ
    from eye to eye and visit to visit. A solver step is parameterised by $\Delta t$, so each step can use the
    actual interval between two visits instead of a fixed one.
\end{itemize}


Because the governing equation is unknown, the update has to be learned
from data, as in the neural PDE solvers
of~\S\ref{sec:background:neural-pde}. These solvers differ
mainly in their built-in assumptions: how far a single prediction can look
across the domain, whether it works on a regular grid or on a graph, and
whether physical terms such as transport are represented explicitly. For
GA, where the governing equation is unknown and only a few dozen patients
are available for training, these assumptions are likely to matter more
than model size.


These four points motivate the structure but are not evidence that it
fits. They also leave open what kind of spatial operator should compute
the update. The CFL argument suggests that an operator applied once per
visit has to see at least as far as the lesion front moves between
visits. On the anisotropic en-face grid, this distance corresponds to very
different numbers of pixels along the two axes: the spacing between
B-scans is about 21 times larger than the spacing between A-scans
(\S\ref{sec:data:spatial}), so a neighbourhood that is symmetric in pixels
is strongly lopsided in millimetres. Whether a local
stencil, a global operator or a combination of both is needed is
therefore not assumed here. \S\ref{sec:background:neural-pde} introduced
the operator families that are candidates for this role, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} compares them.


\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:background:problem}
% TODO (author review, 2026-10-14): formal problem statement written for
% supervisor review 1 (M3.4); check the notation and wording. Follow-up:
% Section 4.2.1 still defines the same update (Eq. method:residual-update)
% and should refer back to Eq. bg:problem-update.

Each eye of the cohort is observed at a sequence of visits at times
$t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_K$, with between five and thirteen visits per eye
and irregular intervals $\Delta t_k = t_{k+1} - t_k$, measured in years.
At visit $k$, the state of the eye is a tensor
\begin{equation*}
  u_k \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}, \qquad C = 11,\; H = 49,\; W = 1024,
\end{equation*}
defined on the en-face lattice $\Omega = \{1, \dots, H\} \times \{1, \dots, W\}$
(\S\ref{sec:data:state}). Channel~0 is the binary GA mask,
$m_k \in \{0, 1\}^{H \times W}$, and channels 1--10 are the depth maps of
ten retinal layer boundaries. The lattice is anisotropic: neighbouring rows
are $h_y = 121.18$\,\textmu{}m apart and neighbouring columns $h_x = 5.68$\,\textmu{}m,
a ratio of about 21 (\S\ref{sec:data:spatial}). Each eye also carries
patient covariates $\mathbf{z}$, its age and sex (\S\ref{sec:data:covariates}).

The task is to forecast the state at a later visit from the state at an
earlier one. The forecast for the next visit is written as one residual
update,
\begin{equation}
  \hat u_{k+1} \;=\; u_k \;+\; \Delta t_k \, f_\theta\!\left(u_k,\, \Delta t_k,\, \mathbf{z}\right),
  \label{eq:bg:problem-update}
\end{equation}
where $f_\theta$ is a learned map with parameters $\theta$. Throughout this
thesis, $f_\theta$ is called the \emph{operator}. The term is used in its
general sense, a map from the state at one time to the state at a later
time. It is not used in the narrower sense of the neural-operator
literature (\S\ref{sec:background:neural-pde}), where it denotes models
such as the FNO or DeepONet that learn maps between function spaces. A
U-Net and a graph network are operators in the first sense but not in the
second. A forecast over several visits is obtained by applying
\eqref{eq:bg:problem-update} repeatedly to its own output,
\begin{equation*}
  \hat u_{k+n} \;=\; \mathcal{A}_\theta\bigl(\Delta t_{k+n-1}, \dots
  \mathcal{A}_\theta(\Delta t_k, u_k)\bigr), \qquad
  \mathcal{A}_\theta(\Delta t, u) \;=\; u + \Delta t \, f_\theta(u, \Delta t, \mathbf{z}),
\end{equation*}
which is the rollout of \S\ref{sec:background:pde-solvers:time} with a
learned update. The parameters $\theta$ are fitted to the observed visits
of the training eyes. The forecast is judged mainly on the mask: the
predicted mask about one year after a visit is compared with the observed
one in the region where the lesion changed
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol}).

In this notation, the research question of \S\ref{sec:introduction:problem}
reads as follows. The first part asks what form the update and its training
need: how $\Delta t$ enters \eqref{eq:bg:problem-update}, how the rollout
is trained, and with which objective. These choices are the
\emph{framework}. The second part asks which operator $f_\theta$, with
which built-in assumptions, gives the best forecasts on the anisotropic
lattice $\Omega$, once the framework is fixed.
