\section{Visit Schedule and Lesion Scale}
\label{app:dataset:schedule}

The visit schedule and the lesion scale of the cohort are summarised in
Figure~\ref{fig:appendix:cohort}. Most eyes were seen six or seven times,
three in four intervals between visits are 180 days long, and the baseline
lesion area ranges from 0.11 to 20.8\,mm$^2$, with a median of
6.7\,mm$^2$.

\section{Statistical Criteria}
\label{app:full-results:criteria}

% Rewritten 2026-10-18 as a z-test (supervisor review 2, SV-5.2). The U-Net
% value replaces the earlier "1.9 times", which could not be reproduced: the
% seed-42/seed-7 pairs of UNET_final_w5 on folds 0-4 give a pooled single-run
% sd sqrt(mean(d^2)/2) = 0.0191 (NOTES, "U-Net run-to-run spread"); local
% repeats on fold 2 vary less (0.005), fold 2 is a quiet fold. z values:
% images/scripts/appendix_ztests.py.
The noise level of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats} rests on seven
pairs of runs with the same configuration on the same fold, two with the
same seed and five with different seeds. They give a standard deviation of
$\sigma \approx 0.0127$ for the late-epoch change-region Dice of a single
run of a graph network. Same-seed and different-seed repeats spread about
equally, so the seed adds nothing measurable on top of the variation
between runs. The runs of the parameter-matched U-Net vary more: its two
seeds on the five folds give $\sigma \approx 0.019$, about 1.5 times as
much.

Every comparison is measured in two ways. The fold-paired test uses the
mean $\bar{\Delta}$ of the per-fold differences of the late-epoch
change-region Dice. If the two arms did not differ, $\bar{\Delta}$ would
vary only through retraining, with a standard deviation
$\sigma_\Delta = \sqrt{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2} / \sqrt{n}$ over $n$ folds:
about 0.008 for two graph networks over five folds, and about 0.010 when
one arm is the U-Net. The statistic $z = \bar{\Delta} / \sigma_\Delta$ is
compared with a standard normal distribution; $|z| > 1.96$ (two-sided
$p < 0.05$) corresponds to a mean difference above about 0.016, or about
0.020 with a U-Net, and to about 0.036 for a difference on a single fold.
The tables give $\bar{\Delta}$ with its standard error over the folds, the
number of folds on which the first arm is higher, and $z$. The per-eye
test pairs the two arms eye by eye. For each of the 75 validation
eyes, the growth-region Dice at the eye's first rollout step at or beyond
0.95 years (about 347 days) is averaged over the late epochs, and the
tables give the mean difference, its standard error, the number of eyes on
which the first arm is higher and the paired $t$ statistic. Both values are
also recomputed with each fold left out in turn.

A difference is counted as established when $|z| > 1.96$, the per-eye $t$
statistic is about 2.5 or larger in magnitude, and neither value changes
sign when any one fold is left out. For the comparisons that were repeated
at the second seed, the difference also has to hold there. No threshold on
$t$ was fixed in advance; the value of about 2.5 describes how the
comparisons were read and should be taken as descriptive. Three caveats
apply. The standard deviation $\sigma$ is itself estimated from only seven
pairs of runs, and that of the U-Net from five. About fifty comparisons are
tested, so one or two may pass the 5\,\% level by chance. The 75 eyes come
from 51 patients, and the two eyes of one patient are not independent, so
the per-eye standard errors are somewhat optimistic.

\section{Background: MM-PDE}
\label{app:mm-pde:background}

\citet{Hu2024} extend message-passing neural solvers with an adaptive
moving mesh. A uniform mesh spends the same resolution everywhere: it
resolves smooth regions more finely than needed and sharp gradients, such
as wave fronts, too coarsely.

Mesh adaptation methods fall into two families. $h$-adaptation adds and
removes vertices, which changes the topology and the number of degrees of
freedom from step to step; standard graph-network training does not handle
such changes well. $r$-adaptation, or moving-mesh adaptation, moves a fixed
set of nodes and keeps both the node count and the graph topology. The
classical methods of this family are described by~\citet{HuangRussell2011}.
Earlier neural $r$-adaptation methods were supervised: they required
optimal meshes from an expensive classical solver as training targets.

The mesh is guided by a monitor function. \citet{Hu2024} use the
matrix-valued monitor
$M = (1 + \lVert \nabla u \rVert_2 / \alpha_M)\, I$, which is large where
the solution varies sharply. The identity matrix $I$ makes the refinement
the same in every direction, the constant 1 keeps $M$ away from zero so
that nodes do not collapse in smooth regions, and the constant $\alpha_M$
sets the balance between adaptivity and numerical stability. The mesh
density is $\rho(x) = \sqrt{\det M(x)}$. This form follows from an upper
bound on the interpolation error of finite elements.

The aim of the adaptation is equidistribution. A coordinate map produces
an $M$-uniform mesh if every cell carries the same integral of $\rho$,
namely $\sigma / N$, where $\sigma$ is the integral of $\rho$ over the
domain $\Omega$ and $N$ the number of cells. Cells therefore shrink where
$M$ is large.

Many maps satisfy this condition, so the one closest to the identity is
chosen, which is an optimal transport problem. By Brenier's theorem its
solution is the gradient $\nabla\phi$ of a convex scalar potential $\phi$.
Substituting this map into the equidistribution condition gives the
Monge--Amp\`ere equation
\[
  \det H(\phi) \;=\; \frac{\sigma}{|\Omega| \, \rho(\nabla \phi)},
\]
a fully non-linear second-order equation for $\phi$, with the condition
that the boundary of the domain maps onto itself. If $\phi$ is convex, the
map $\nabla\phi$ has a non-singular Jacobian, so the mesh cannot fold.
Convexity of a scalar potential is easier to encourage in training than a
non-folding condition on a vector field, and for this reason the potential
is learned instead of the map. It is written as
$\phi(x) = \tfrac12 \lVert x \rVert^2 + \psi(x)$, so that
$\nabla\phi = x + \nabla\psi$. The network predicts only $\psi$, whose
gradient is the displacement of the nodes, and the map is close to the
identity at initialisation.

The data-free mesh mover (DMM) has the form of a DeepONet. A branch network
encodes the state $u$, a trunk network encodes a continuous query
coordinate, and together they give the potential $\phi$. The DMM is
trained without example meshes, on the loss
$L = L_{\text{eq}} + \beta L_{\text{bound}} + \gamma L_{\text{convex}}$,
where $L_{\text{eq}}$ is the squared residual of the Monge--Amp\`ere
equation at sampled interior points, $L_{\text{bound}}$ enforces the
boundary condition and $L_{\text{convex}}$ penalises violations of
convexity. Without the convexity term, a small equation residual can still
come with a folded mesh. Training uses Adam, followed by BFGS on the last
layer. The DMM is trained in advance and kept frozen while the solver is
trained. This separates the geometric problem from the forecasting
objective and lets the same mesh mover serve several experiments.

The solver combines two branches,
$\mathcal{M}(u_t) = G_1(u_t) + \mathcal{I}(G_2, \nabla\phi, u_t)$. Here
$G_1$ is a graph network on the uniform mesh, $G_2$ a graph network on the
moved mesh, and $\mathcal{I}$ a learned interpolation that maps the result
of $G_2$ back to the uniform mesh. The uniform branch covers the whole
domain at uniform resolution, and the moved branch adds a correction where
the gradients are steep. The interpolation network, ItpNet, computes its
weights from the geometry of the moved mesh instead of using fixed
finite-element basis functions. It is pretrained with its own optimiser
before the two branches are trained together, because an untrained
interpolation would pass meaningless values to the uniform branch.

\citet{Hu2024} report four ablations of the dual-branch solver: removing
the uniform branch, replacing the moved branch by a second branch on the
original mesh, removing the residual-cut network of their model (not used
in this thesis), and replacing the DMM mesh by the uniform mesh. Each of
them increases the error. They conclude that the branch on the original
mesh, the residual cut and a well-defined moving mesh are all needed for
accuracy. The original method thus has two parts that a GA version has to
provide: a mesh mover for the GA state and a dual-branch composition.

\label{app:mm-pde:extension}

\subsection{Data-Free Mesh Mover for GA}
\label{app:mm-pde:dmm}

The mesh mover reads only channel~0, the binary GA mask, on the native
$49 \times 1024$ grid. At the first step of a rollout this is the observed
mask. At later steps it is the model's own predicted mask, denormalised and
thresholded back to binary, so the mesh follows the predicted lesion as it
would in use. Only the mesh mover reads the mask alone; both solver
branches process all eleven channels. An earlier version was trained on a
square $256 \times 256$ mask sampled from the fundus image. The native grid
replaced it, which removes a resizing step from every step of the rollout.

The architecture follows the DeepONet form \citep{Lu2021} as adapted
by~\citet{Hu2024}. A convolutional encoder, the branch network of the
DeepONet, reads the mask with three stride-2 convolutions
($1 \to 16 \to 32 \to 64$ channels), adaptive average pooling to
$4 \times 4$ and a linear layer to a 512-dimensional latent vector. Because
of the pooling, it accepts any grid size. The trunk network, a tanh
multilayer perceptron with widths 2, 32 and 512, reads the continuous
coordinate $\xi \in [0,1]^2$. A head maps the concatenated outputs of
encoder and trunk to the scalar potential $\phi$ through widths 1024, 512,
512 and 1, one layer deeper than the base head. The moved mesh is
$x(\xi) = \xi + \nabla\phi(\xi)$, computed by automatic differentiation.
The mesh mover has $1\,394\,273$ parameters. They are frozen during solver
training and excluded from the optimiser.

The monitor differs from the one of~\S\ref{app:mm-pde:background}. It is a
scalar,
\begin{equation}
  M \;=\; 1 + \log\!\Big(1 + \frac{\lVert \nabla u \rVert}
  {\alpha_{\text{scale}}\, \bar g + \epsilon}\Big),
  \label{eq:appendix:monitor}
\end{equation}
where $u$ is the mask after a Gaussian blur, $\bar g$ the mean gradient
magnitude over the grid, $\alpha_{\text{scale}} = 0.1$ and
$\epsilon = 10^{-10}$. The gradient is taken in the unit-square
computational coordinate, which gives the two axes their correct relative
weight on the anisotropic grid. Two parts of this monitor were necessary.
The blur ($\sigma = 1.0$\,px) is applied to the monitor only; the encoder
always receives the raw binary mask. Without the blur, the step of the
binary mask at the lesion edge made the first GA run diverge to NaN. On the
native grid, the unblurred monitor no longer diverges but leaves the mesh
almost unmoved (\S\ref{app:mm-pde:mesh-quality}). The logarithm bounds the
ratio between the monitor at the lesion border and its value elsewhere, so
the equidistribution target remains attainable. Two further ideas were
tested and left out. A blur kernel stretched along the columns to match the
physical spacing gave no gain ($p = 0.95$) and folded the mesh
intermittently, and a penalty on the Jacobian determinant made folding
worse.

The loss has three terms with weights 1, 1000 and 1. The first is the
Monge--Amp\`ere residual at sampled interior points,
$\operatorname{MSE}\big(M(\xi + \nabla\phi)\,\det(I + \nabla^2\phi) /
\bar M,\ 1\big)$. It requires the monitor at the moved point times the
change of cell area to be the same everywhere. The second, a soft boundary
term, penalises the normal derivative of $\phi$ on the four edges, so that
boundary nodes stay on the boundary. The third penalises negative diagonal
entries of the Hessian of $\phi$, an early sign of folding. This term stays
close to zero throughout training. Validity is enforced instead when a
checkpoint is selected, by requiring zero folded cells. The last-layer BFGS
refinement of~\citet{Hu2024} was not used.

Each iteration samples 5 masks and 1000 points per mask, 90\,\% of them in
proportion to the monitor and 10\,\% uniformly. The mesh mover was trained
on all 478 masks of the cohort, the start mask of every training window of
all 75 eyes. Because it never sees a future state, this leaks no label, and
the same mesh mover is in-distribution for every fold. It was trained with
Adam, learning rate $2 \times 10^{-4}$ and weight decay $10^{-5}$, for 500
epochs, with the learning rate multiplied by 0.2 at epochs 333 and 467. The
configuration was selected from a 55-run search on the native grid. Mesh
quality (\S\ref{app:mm-pde:mesh-quality}) improved with the number of
epochs up to 500. The two settings with the largest effect were the extra
head layer and the sharper blur of $\sigma = 1$ instead of 2, and they
helped only in combination. The architecture of the encoder made no
measurable difference across five variants; the pooling encoder is kept
because it accepts any grid size. Table~\ref{tab:appendix:dmm-config}

\subsection{Dual-Branch Composition}
\label{app:mm-pde:dual}

The dual-branch model predicts
\begin{equation}
  \hat u_{t+\Delta t} \;=\; u_t + \Delta t\, f_\theta(u_t)
  \;+\; \alpha \cdot \tilde I\big[\Delta t\, g_\theta(u_t)\big],
  \label{eq:appendix:dual}
\end{equation}
where $f_\theta$ runs on the uniform grid and $g_\theta$ on the moved mesh.
The uniform branch alone gives the full next state,
$u_t + \Delta t\, f_\theta$. The correction branch gives an increment
scaled by $\Delta t$, which the interpolation $\tilde I$ carries back to
the uniform grid. Both branches are the graph network
of~\S\ref{sec:method:mppde}, with two message-passing rounds of width 64.

% TODO: cite Bachlechner2021 (ReZero, UAI 2021, arXiv:2003.04887) once the
% PDF is filed and the entry added to references.bib.
The gate $\alpha$ is a learnable scalar initialised to zero, the ReZero
construction of Bachlechner et al. (2021). At the start, the dual model is
therefore identical to the uniform branch alone, and the correction enters
the prediction only if training increases $|\alpha|$. In the following,
the gate is said to open when $|\alpha|$ grows well beyond zero and to stay
shut when it remains close to zero. The gate also fixes the scale of the
correction. The loss constrains only the sum of the two branches, whose
outputs could otherwise grow large and cancel each other. The gate has its
own optimiser group without weight decay. Unlike every operator of the
main text, the decoder of the correction branch is not zero-initialised.
If both the decoder and $\alpha$ started at zero, the gradients of both
would be exactly zero, and the correction branch could never become
active. The zero start of the dual model therefore comes from $\alpha$
alone.

The moved nodes lie off the lattice, where the dilated stencil
of~\S\ref{sec:method:graph} is not defined. The correction branch therefore
builds its own $k$-nearest-neighbour graph ($k = 12$) on the moved
positions, searched in index space, that is, with the coordinates divided
by the grid spacing. A search in physical millimetres would return only
neighbours from the same row on this grid. The implementation did so until
6 August 2026, and dual-branch results from before that date are not used.

The interpolation is a learned network (ItpNet). For each query point, a
tanh multilayer perceptron with widths 26, 128, 64 and 12 and a linear
output turns the coordinates of its 12 nearest source points into 12
weights. Two independent copies are used. One interpolates all eleven
channels from the uniform grid to the moved mesh, as the input of the
correction branch, and the other carries the correction back. The weights
of the return direction are renormalised to sum to one at each point, with
a sign-preserving floor of 0.1 on the denominator, which bounds the gain at
10. The floor is active at about 12.6\,\% of the points. ItpNet is
pretrained at epoch 0 for 25 passes with its own AdamW optimiser
\citep{Loshchilov2019}, on the round trip uniform $\to$ moved $\to$
uniform, which should return the input unchanged. The pretraining uses the
channel-weighted mean squared error only, because the soft-Dice term would
push the interpolation away from the identity it is meant to learn. During
the pretraining, the gradients are clipped and the gate is disabled.

The mesh is recomputed from the current state at every step, because a
mesh built at the start of a multi-step window would no longer match the
lesion at the later steps. The dual-branch model therefore predicts one
step at a time. This is a second reason for one-step prediction, in
addition to the short visit sequences of~\S\ref{sec:method:framework}.

Gradients are clipped to a norm of 1.0 separately in five groups: the
uniform branch, the correction branch, the ItpNet weights, the gate
$\alpha$ and the layer encoder; the mesh mover is frozen. In the dual runs,
the gradients of ItpNet have a median of $10^3$ to $10^4$, against about 1
for the uniform branch. Under one joint clip they would dominate the joint
norm and shrink the updates of the uniform branch accordingly.

The dual-branch model has $175\,663$ trainable parameters against
$75\,339$ for the single-branch model in the configuration of the
five-fold runs, about $100\,000$ more for the second network and ItpNet.
With the dilated stencil the counts are $159\,279$ and $67\,147$. The
frozen mesh mover is not counted.

The bypass control loads the same mesh mover and runs the second network
and the full ItpNet round trip, but places the correction branch on the
uniform grid instead of the moved mesh. It has the same parameters as the
dual model and differs from it only in the mesh geometry. The
Runge--Kutta wrapper of~\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}, the dense operators
and the finite-element networks exist only as single-branch models, so
they are compared with the single-branch graph network and not with the
dual branch. In total, the extension adds a frozen mesh mover, a second
graph network, two interpolation networks and a gate to the
single-branch model.

\section{Moving Mesh Quality}
\label{app:mm-pde:mesh-quality}

The mesh mover was judged on its own terms, independently of the
prediction, so that a defective mesh can be told apart from a correction
branch that does not use a good one. Four metrics were used. All of them
depend only on the mask and use the same reference for every run. The
number of tangled cells counts the mesh cells with a non-positive signed
area, that is, folds. The edge gain measures whether nodes move to the
lesion border. The border is widened to a band of half-width 0.02 of the
domain, normalised to a grid mean of 1, and averaged at the moved node
positions. The unmoved mesh scores exactly 1, and a higher value means more
nodes on the border. The reference coefficient of variation,
\texttt{cov\_ref}, is the standard deviation divided by the mean of the
cell areas weighted by one fixed reference monitor, the equidistribution
measure of~\citet{HuangRussell2011}; lower values mean a more even mesh.
The determinant margin, \texttt{det\_min}, is the minimum of
$\det(I + \nabla^2\phi)$ over random points. A positive value means that
the map folds nowhere, not only at the grid nodes.

\section{Result}
\label{app:mm-pde:result}

compared over the five folds at seed 42, with the evaluation protocol of
the main text (\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}) and the criteria
of~\S\ref{app:full-results:criteria}. All three arms use the earlier
$k$-nearest-neighbour graph network with mean and maximum aggregation. This
is neither the canonical dilated stencil nor the $k$-nearest-neighbour arm

0.4623. Table~\ref{tab:appendix:dual-result} lists the arms.

The dual branch gives no measurable gain. Against the bypass control, the
fold-paired difference is $-0.0001 \pm 0.0060$~SE (higher on 3 of 5 folds)
and the per-eye difference $+0.0001 \pm 0.0038$ (33 of 75 eyes). This is
the tightest null result of the project: its standard error is less than
half the difference of 0.016 that a five-fold paired mean must exceed.
Against the single branch, the difference is $-0.0013 \pm 0.0086$~SE (3 of
5 folds) and per eye $-0.0018 \pm 0.0064$ (32 of 75). The dual branch
costs about ten times the training time per epoch of the single branch.

The gate stays shut, with and without the moving mesh. In every fold of
both arms, $\alpha$ moves from zero to exactly $\pm 0.0020$ in the first
step, one optimiser step from the zero start, as designed. It then peaks at
0.058 to 0.093 in the dual branch and at 0.031 to 0.101 in the bypass
control, averages 0.009 to 0.024 and 0.005 to 0.023 in absolute value, and
ends near zero in every fold. The range of the peaks in the bypass control
encloses that of the dual branch, and the largest peak of either arm,
0.1005, belongs to a bypass fold.

A probe on the trained dual model explains why a gate near zero stays
there. The gradient that reaches the correction branch is proportional to
$\alpha$. Its norm is 0 at $\alpha = 0$, 0.0034 at $\alpha = 0.002$ and
1.799 at $\alpha = 1$, against about 2.09 for the uniform branch. At the
$|\alpha| \approx 0.02$ that the runs reach, the correction branch receives
a learning signal about 60 times weaker than the uniform branch. While the
gate is near zero, the correction branch therefore learns little, and its
output gives the training no reason to increase $\alpha$.

Three further runs on fold~2 tested this reading, and in none of them did
the correction branch engage. Without the gate, with the decoder
zero-initialised instead, the change-region Dice falls to 0.3148 against
0.4878 for the gated dual model. This loss of 0.173 is about five times the
single-fold noise floor of 0.036, and the gradient of the correction branch
is still zero at the median. The gate therefore keeps an untrained
correction from being added at full weight. Without weight decay on the
correction branch and ItpNet, the Dice is 0.0092 higher than its
counterpart, under the single-fold floor. The gate peaks lower (0.058
against 0.067), and the parameters of the correction branch grow instead
of shrinking, so weight decay does not keep the gate shut. On the dilated
stencil, the dual model scores 0.5754 against 0.6060 for its own
single-branch counterpart, a difference of $-0.031$ (per eye
$-0.0305 \pm 0.0086$, 3 of 16 eyes), at roughly nine times the cost. The
gain of the stencil carries over to the dual model, but the correction
branch still does not engage.

Before the runs, $\alpha \to 0$ had been fixed as a reportable outcome, to
be read as evidence that mesh adaptation does not help GA forecasting. The
bypass control, part of the same design, shows that this reading is not
valid, because its gate behaves in the same way without any mesh. The
experiments support a narrower statement. The gated correction branch did
not engage in any tested variant: on the moved mesh and on the uniform
grid, without weight decay, and without the gate. The dual-branch model
gave no measurable gain at about ten times the cost. This statement
concerns this implementation and not mesh adaptation for GA in general.
\citet{Hu2024} report that their dual branch improves on the uniform grid
on their benchmarks. The probe explains why a gate near zero stays there,
but the experiments here do not show why the correction branch fails to
engage in the first place.

\section{Outlook}
\label{app:mm-pde:outlook}

% Moved here from Section 6.4 (author review v5 #11, 2026-10-01).
The monitor of the mesh mover is computed from the current state, whereas
the change happens in the next one. A monitor built on a surrogate of the
next state, the open problem named by~\citet{Hu2024}, would place the nodes
where the lesion is about to grow. Whether it would also help the
correction branch to engage is open, since the branch did not engage even
on the uniform grid.
