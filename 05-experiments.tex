\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol}

This chapter answers the second part of the research question: what kind
of operator the framework of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} needs. Many operators
are run through the same slot under identical conditions. This section
defines how they are scored and how two scores are compared.

\subsection{Why Not Per-Pixel Error}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol:mse}

GA progresses slowly. Between two consecutive visits, almost all pixels of
the lesion mask stay unchanged. Persistence, which predicts no change at
all, is therefore a strong per-pixel baseline. A per-pixel mean squared
error or root mean squared error (RMSE) is dominated by the static pixels,
and a model can reach a low per-pixel error while predicting no
progression at all.

The same holds for a Dice score over the whole lesion. The static interior
of the lesion dominates it, so persistence already reaches a full-mask Dice
of about 0.87. Full-mask Dice is therefore almost blind to progression.

Raw errors are also not compared across architectures. The clinical
metrics are computed on the thresholded mask. The dense operators, such as
the U-Net and the Fourier Neural Operator, decalibrate their raw mask
regression under autoregressive rollout much more than the graph network
does: the late rollout mask RMSE of the U-Net is roughly twice that of the
graph network, while its thresholded metrics are higher. A raw-error
comparison across architecture classes would therefore measure calibration,
not prediction. The soft-Dice loss adds a separate, known stretch to the
raw mask values (\S\ref{sec:method:training:loss}). RMSE values are
computed and logged, but no RMSE value is used as evidence in this chapter.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol:metrics}

The headline metric is the change-region Dice at the one-year anchor. All
predicted and ground-truth masks are binarised at the physical threshold of
0.5, mapped into normalised space in the same way as in the loss. Let $b$
be the thresholded mask at the baseline visit, the first visit of the
rollout, $p$ the thresholded prediction at the anchor and $y$ the
thresholded ground truth at the anchor. The predicted and the true change
regions are
\begin{equation}
  \hat{C} = \{\, i : p_i \neq b_i \,\}, \qquad
  C = \{\, i : y_i \neq b_i \,\},
  \label{eq:experiments:change-region}
\end{equation}
and the change-region Dice is $2\,|\hat{C} \cap C| \,/\, (|\hat{C}| + |C|)$.
A Dice score measures the overlap of two regions: it is 1 when they
coincide and 0 when they share no pixel. Applied to the change regions, it
asks whether the model predicts change where change actually happened, and
it ignores the large part of the lesion that stays the same.

Three consequences follow. First, persistence predicts no change, so its
score is 0 by construction, and every positive value is lift over "no
progression". Second, the metric is symmetric. A predicted shrinkage counts
as predicted change and is scored against the truth, which is consistent
with the absence of a monotonic-growth constraint
(\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}). Third, it is not the growth-region Dice
of \citet{Mai2024}, which this chapter also uses. The growth-region Dice
scores only new atrophy, the pixels that are lesion at the anchor but not
at baseline. The change-region Dice scores every changed pixel, growth and
shrinkage alike. Both score 0 for persistence and are easy to confuse:
Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms} and all differences between arms use the
change-region Dice, while the eye counts of
\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats} use the growth-region Dice. The
change-region Dice is also the model-selection metric.

\subsection{Rollout and Reporting}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol:rollout}

Two evaluation procedures run after every epoch on the validation eyes of
the fold. The one-step evaluation predicts every validation window once
from its observed input. It isolates the accuracy of a single step from the
drift of a rollout. The rollout evaluation is the procedure every reported
number comes from. For each validation eye, it starts at the baseline
visit and predicts the next visit. The prediction is then fed back as the
input of the next window, and the rollout continues over the eye's real
visit schedule, with the actual $\Delta t$ of each window.

The one-year anchor is taken at the first step whose cumulative time
reaches it (\S\ref{sec:data:temporal}). For a few eyes this step lies
beyond one year, where the true change region is larger; this is accepted,
as \citet{Mai2024} also score every follow-up visit by its time since
baseline rather than at a fixed horizon. Persistence is computed inside every
rollout by the same code; for the anchor metrics it is the baseline mask
held constant. Per-step metrics are averaged per eye first and then over
the eyes, so every eye counts once, whatever its number of visits.

The checkpoint kept as the best one is the epoch with the highest
change-region Dice at the anchor. The reported numbers, however, are not
best-epoch values, for two reasons. First, the metric is noisy: with 12 to
20 validation eyes per fold, it still fluctuates from epoch to epoch once
training has levelled off, with a standard deviation of about 0.01 to 0.05
within one run (Figure~\ref{fig:experiments:curves}a), so the highest epoch
is mostly the luckiest one.

\subsection{Noise Level and Comparisons}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}

Two sources of variation enter every comparison. The first is training
noise. Repeated runs of the same configuration do not give identical
results on the GPU (\S\ref{sec:method:implementation}): retraining the same
model on the same fold already changes its late-epoch score by up to about
0.02. The second is the fold. The five folds hold different eyes, and the
spread between them is larger than most differences between arms
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}). Two arms are therefore always
compared fold by fold, on the same five folds, and the five differences
are averaged.

Differences of about 0.02 or less are within the noise level and are not
interpreted on their own. A difference is treated as real only when its
average over the five folds clearly exceeds what retraining alone
produces, when it holds on most folds and most of the 75 validation eyes,
and when it does not depend on a single fold. Results are stated in these
terms, for example ``higher by 0.064 on average, on all five folds and on
70 of the 75 eyes''. The eye counts use the growth-region Dice at the
anchor, because the change-region Dice was logged only as a mean over the
eyes of each fold. In tables, ``mean $\pm$ sd'' is the mean and the
standard deviation over the five fold values, each the late-epoch mean of
one run.

The main comparisons were repeated with a second random seed, and the
conclusions drawn in this chapter hold at both. All numbers in this
chapter are for the first seed. Appendix~\ref{app:full-results:criteria}
gives the exact criteria, and Appendix~\ref{app:full-results:seeds} the
standard errors and test statistics of every comparison together with the
values at the second seed.

The three highest arms come from three unrelated architecture classes: a
finite-element network, a graph network and a U-Net. The T-FEN is higher
than the dilated-stencil graph network by 0.017 on average, on four of the
five folds and on 46 of the 75 eyes: a small lead at the noise level. The
dilated-stencil network is higher than the U-Net by 0.021 on average, but
on only three of the five folds; the scores of the U-Net vary more between
runs than those of the graph networks
(Appendix~\ref{app:full-results:criteria}), so this is at the noise
level, and the two cannot be told apart. The T-FEN is higher than
the U-Net by 0.038 on average, on all five folds and on 51 of the 75 eyes,
and this lead is real.

The three highest arms thus lie within about 0.04 of each other. The
T-FEN is highest and lies above the U-Net; the dilated-stencil network and
the U-Net cannot be told apart. The two highest arms are the two that
carry a remedy for the reach deficit taken up
in~\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:convergence}: the transport term and
the dilated stencil. What separates the arms is therefore not their class
but the ingredients they carry.

\section{The Ingredient Study}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients}

The strongest arms of different classes lie within about 0.04 of each
other, so comparing whole architectures says little about what makes an
operator work. This section therefore compares operators that differ in one
property only, with the rest of the framework held fixed. Each comparison
measures one ingredient. All comparisons are taken fold by fold over the
same five folds (\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}).

\subsection{Spatial Context}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients:context}

The locality ladder uses the graph network on the index-space
$k$-nearest-neighbour graph with zero, one and two message-passing rounds
(\S\ref{sec:method:family:floors}). With zero rounds, the per-pixel floor
reads 0.0000 on every fold, identical to persistence. With one round, the
one-hop floor reads $0.4515 \pm 0.0243$. With two rounds, the $k$-NN arm
reads $0.4623 \pm 0.0361$. The second round adds 0.011 on average, within
the noise level: the one-hop floor is higher on one of the five folds and
on 33 of the 75 eyes. The second round thus adds at most about 0.01.

A single ring of neighbours therefore buys almost everything the graph
network on this graph achieves, and the second ring adds little or nothing. Every
point of change-region Dice comes from spatial context. One possible reason
why the second ring adds so little is where its extra reach lies: on the
index-space graph it extends mostly across rows, and only about 0.01\,mm
along them (\S\ref{sec:method:graph}). This motivates the
next comparison, which changes where the neighbours lie.

\subsection{Physical Reach at Lesion Scale}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients:reach}

This comparison keeps the graph network, the loss and the data, and changes
only the edge set, from the index-space $k$-nearest-neighbour graph to the
dilated stencil (\S\ref{sec:method:graph}, Figure~\ref{fig:method:stencils}).
Both networks use mean
aggregation and have the same 67\,147 parameters. The dilated stencil is
higher by 0.064 on average, on all five folds and on 70 of the 75 eyes.
This gain was obtained by changing only the edge set, at the same parameter
count, and it is the reason the dilated stencil is the canonical
configuration.

Table~\ref{tab:experiments:reach} compares other stencil geometries with the
default. Both a shorter and a longer reach along the rows are lower: by
0.019 for a reach of $\pm 12$ columns and by 0.026 for $\pm 42$ columns,
each higher than the default on at most one fold. The default of $\pm 21$
columns, about 0.12\,mm, is therefore the best of the tested reaches on
this axis. It lies close to the 90th percentile of the
per-visit front advance, 23 columns (\S\ref{sec:method:graph}). The
$\pm 42$ geometry keeps the 20 neighbours of the default by doubling the
column spacing from 7 to 14, so its neighbours are sparser. The geometry
reaching $\pm 35$ columns keeps the default spacing of 7 columns instead,
with 32 neighbours; it is also lower, by 0.010, within the noise level. A
longer reach therefore does not help even when the spacing is kept, so the
penalty at $\pm 42$ columns is not a matter of sparser neighbours alone.
Widening the stencil across rows, to $\pm 2$ rows, costs the most of all
geometries, 0.039.

index-space graph on one axis of physical reach.

A higher degree at the same reach buys nothing. The index-space
$k$-nearest-neighbour graph with $k = 20$ reaches as far as with $k = 12$
but has more neighbours. Against the same network with $k = 12$, it is
lower by 0.010 on average, higher on only one of the five folds and on 20
of the 75 eyes. The fold average is within the noise level while the eyes
lean consistently one way, so the difference is not established; neither
view points to a gain from the extra neighbours. What matters is where the
neighbours are placed, not how many there are.

This also explains an earlier result. Before the dilated stencil, the
parameter-matched U-Net was higher than the $k$-NN graph network, by
0.042 on average, on four of the five folds and on 53 of the 75 eyes.
Fold 3 goes the other way ($-0.042$), so the effect varies between folds.
With the
dilated stencil, the graph network is indistinguishable from the U-Net
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}). The deficit of the graph network
was therefore located in its graph --- in where its neighbours lie
physically --- and not in its operator class.

\subsection{Global and Local Context Together}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients:global}

The Fourier Neural Operator has global spectral support but no local path
(\S\ref{sec:method:family:dense}). The local-kernel hybrid adds a
$3 \times 3$ local path to the same operator, at a cost of 800 parameters.

The FNO is higher than the $k$-NN graph network by 0.021 on average, on
three of the five folds and on 41 of the 75 eyes. Fold 4 carries all of
it: without fold 4 the average is $-0.001$, so this difference is not
established. Against the U-Net, the FNO is lower by 0.022 on average and
higher on only one of the five folds and on 30 of the 75 eyes: at the
one-year anchor the FNO lies below the U-Net. Beyond one year the order
changes. In
the growth-region Dice by horizon, the FNO reads 0.546, 0.583 and 0.594 for
one to two, two to three and more than three years, and the U-Net 0.563,
0.581 and 0.555: the FNO trails the U-Net in the second year, ties it in
the third and lies above it beyond three years
(Figure~\ref{fig:experiments:horizon}a). Global spectral support
may therefore matter more at long horizons than at the one-year anchor;
the horizon values are descriptive and were not tested for significance.

The hybrid is higher than the $k$-NN graph network by 0.036 on average,

\subsection{Two Routes to One Deficit}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients:convergence}

The GA front moves about 10 to 25 columns per visit, with a median of 12
and a 90th percentile of 23 columns (\S\ref{sec:method:graph}). One hop on
the index-space graph reaches only $\pm 2$ columns, about 0.011\,mm along a
row. An operator of that kind cannot see as far as the lesion front moves
between two visits.

Two unrelated constructions correct this deficit. The first is physical
reach: the dilated stencil, whose single hop spans 21 columns. The second
is an explicit transport term: the T-FEN, established against a
parameter-matched control (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:transport}).
Each is
established against its own control, and the two end up within about
0.017 of each other, at the noise level; they are the two highest arms of
Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms}
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}). The central
architectural result of the survey is this coincidence --- two independent
routes to the same deficit --- rather than the score of any single arm. It
is suggestive rather than a proof of mechanism.

The architecture class therefore is not what separates the strongest
operators; the ingredients they carry are. A third candidate, global and
local context together, is not established
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:global}) and is not counted among
them. \S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations} reports what
was measured and did not matter.


\section{Negative Results}
\label{sec:experiments:ablations}

A negative result here is a comparison whose difference stays within the
noise level of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}. It is a null at the
available precision. It does not show that the tested
alternative is equivalent, or that the mechanism behind it has no effect,
and each finding is scoped to the backbone, the folds and the seed on which
it was measured. This section reports three groups of such results: the
time handling of the update (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:time}), the
composition of the training objective (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:loss})
and the capacity of the operators
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:capacity}).

\subsection{Time Handling}
\label{sec:experiments:ablations:time}

The framework handles time in a deliberately simple way
(\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}): the operator makes one step over the
whole interval to the next visit, and $\Delta t$ enters twice, as an input
of the operator and as a multiplier of its output,
$u_{t+\Delta t} = u_t + \Delta t \cdot f_\theta(u_t, \Delta t, z)$. This
design is part of the framework that every operator shares, so it matters
whether its details carry any of the accuracy. Two tests ask this, both on
the canonical dilated-stencil network.

The first test asks whether the multiplication by $\Delta t$ is needed.
The multiplication was removed, so the update became
$u_t + f_\theta(u_t, \Delta t, z)$, while $\Delta t$ remained an input of
the operator. The change-region Dice does not change: $-0.0006$ on average,
higher on two of the five folds and on 30 of the 75 eyes.
% Values: thesis_numbers.py pair ANISOGNN_final_dilmean_nodts
% ANISOGNN_final_dilmean, 2026-10-01 (arm 0.5252 +/- 0.0425 vs 0.5258).
% The k-NN run of this ablation is deliberately not reported (author).
The reason lies in the mask channel. Its two values are a fixed distance
apart in normalised units, $-0.602$ and $+1.660$
(\S\ref{sec:data:normalization}). At a pixel that changes between two
visits, the scaled update therefore has to produce exactly that distance
divided by $\Delta t$, whatever the interval, and the operator simply
learns to divide by $\Delta t$ internally. The multiplication is a change
of parameterisation, not additional knowledge. It is kept because it makes
the prediction fall back to the current state as $\Delta t$ approaches zero
(\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}), and because it is the form MP-PDE uses
\citep{Brandstetter2022}. It also means that $f_\theta$ is not a growth
rate that could be read off; whether the operator can be read as a
continuous-time model at all is tested
in~\S\ref{sec:experiments:validity:swap}.

The second test asks whether the time-stepping scheme matters. The single
step was replaced by a classical
fourth-order Runge--Kutta step (\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}), which
evaluates the operator four times within the interval and combines the
results. In this setting the operator does not receive $\Delta t$ as an
input (the autonomous field of \S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}); time enters
only through the integrator, which steps over the
interval. Over the dilated-stencil network, the change-region Dice is 0.012
lower than with the single step, on four of the five folds; over the FNO it
is 0.006 higher. Both differences lie within the noise level, and the Runge--Kutta
step costs about 4.8 times the training time (\S\ref{sec:experiments:cost}).
% Values: ANISOGNN_final_oderk4n1 - ANISOGNN_final_dilmean -0.0119 +/-
% 0.0037 SE (1/5 higher), per eye -0.0132 +/- 0.0055 (28/75);
% FNO_final_w5rk4 - FNO_final_w5 +0.0062 +/- 0.0056 SE (4/5), per eye
% +0.0064 +/- 0.0069 (43/75). The fold-2 decomposition (wrapper offset,
% Delta t input removed, order) was dropped, author review v4 #33.

Together, the two tests show that neither the multiplication by
$\Delta t$ nor the integration scheme changes the result. What the update needs is that the length of the interval
reaches the operator at all, either as an input to a single step or through
an integrator that steps over the interval; how it arrives makes no
measurable difference. The simple update of the framework is therefore
sufficient, and the accuracy is decided by the spatial operator
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients}), not by the way time is stepped.
% Note: no model without any time information was trained; "needs the
% interval at all" is argued (a 3-month and a 2-year gap must give different
% predictions), not measured.

\subsection{The Objective}
\label{sec:experiments:ablations:loss}

Every operator is trained with the same objective
(\S\ref{sec:method:training:loss}): a mean squared error in which the mask
channel is weighted five times more than each layer channel, plus a
soft-Dice term on the mask channel, with no monotonic-growth penalty. Like
the time handling, this objective is part of the shared framework, so it
matters which of its parts carry the result. Three runs on the canonical
dilated-stencil network ask this: one without any emphasis on the mask,
one without the soft-Dice term, and one with a monotonic-growth penalty
added.

The first run tests whether the mask needs any emphasis at all. Almost
every pixel of the mask stays the same between two visits. With an
unweighted mean squared error, a model can therefore reach a low loss by
copying its input, which predicts no change at all and scores 0 on the
change-region Dice. The collapse counter of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:metrics}
detects this. Trained with a plain, unweighted mean squared error and no
soft-Dice term, the network falls into this collapse and never leaves it
within the 30 epochs: its change-region Dice is 0 on every fold and at
every epoch, and at the last epoch 74 of the 75 validation eyes have a
prediction identical to their baseline mask. The objective, not the
operator, decides whether the model learns any change at all.

The second run keeps the mask weighting and removes only the soft-Dice
term. The network then does leave the collapse, but late: on the five folds, its
change-region Dice first rises above 0 at epochs 2, 8, 3, 7 and 12, against
epoch 0 with the full objective (Figure~\ref{fig:experiments:curves}b).
It also ends lower, by 0.050 on average, on all five folds and on 56 of
the 75 eyes. The largest part comes from fold 4, where the collapse lasts
longest: the difference is $-0.114$ there and $-0.012$ to $-0.044$ on the
other folds. The same fold makes the run less steady. It leaves the
collapse only at epoch 12, inside the late-epoch window, so averaged over
the folds the epoch-to-epoch standard deviation of the late-epoch Dice is
0.043 against 0.015 with the full objective, but only 0.017 against 0.013
on folds 0 to 3. The weighting is therefore what lets the model escape the
collapse, and the soft-Dice term adds an immediate escape and a higher
score.

The third run adds a monotonic-growth penalty. Atrophic tissue does not
regenerate, so a penalty on any decrease of the mask where the previous
state is atrophic is a natural prior. It is implemented
(\S\ref{sec:method:training:loss}) but switched off in every other run.
With the penalty at weight 2.0, the change-region Dice is $-0.004$ on
average, higher on two of the five folds and on 34 of the 75 eyes: no
measurable effect, so the simpler objective is kept.
% Values: thesis_numbers.py pair ANISOGNN_final_dilmean_mono2
% ANISOGNN_final_dilmean, 2026-10-01 (arm 0.5221 +/- 0.0432).
Two arguments speak against the penalty independently of its effect on the
score. Under the pushforward curriculum
(\S\ref{sec:method:training:curriculum}), the previous state on an unrolled
step is the model's own earlier prediction, so the penalty locks in the
model's own false positives: a pixel wrongly marked as atrophic can no
longer be corrected toward the ground truth without paying the penalty. A
direct computation with the training loss on one such pixel, at penalty
weight 2.0, gives a penalty gradient 31.8 times that of the data terms, so
the total gradient points away from the ground truth. The penalty therefore enforces different
priors on teacher-forced and on unrolled steps. In addition, the reference
segmentations themselves shrink locally between most pairs of visits
(\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}), which a strict growth constraint would
contradict.

\subsection{Capacity}
\label{sec:experiments:ablations:capacity}

Every architecture arm of Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms} runs at 67 to 83
thousand parameters (\S\ref{sec:method:family:params}). Whether the
comparisons of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}
and~\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients} depend on that size was tested
separately. Each architecture class was scaled while everything else was
held fixed: the same loss, curriculum, epoch budget and five folds, seed 42.
Each run changes one size setting only. Width is the setting compared
across classes, because it adds parameters without changing how far one
step reaches. Depth does change the reach of the graph network, so it is
read for each class on its own; the U-Net and the FNO have no depth
setting. The design and a decision rule were fixed before the first run: a

\section{Validity Diagnostics}
\label{sec:experiments:validity}

The two diagnostics of this section are not accuracy numbers. They decide
which statements about the operators are licensed. The first tests whether
a trained operator can be read as a continuous-time system; the second
shows why the first version of the T-FEN was not a stable free-running
model, and how the energy-conserving form fixes it.

\subsection{The Solver-Swap Test}
\label{sec:experiments:validity:swap}

The update of the framework, $u_t + \Delta t \cdot f_\theta$, has the form
of one explicit Euler step (\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}), and the
Runge--Kutta wrapper and the Finite Element Network go further and
integrate $f_\theta$ over several sub-steps
(\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}, \S\ref{sec:method:family:fen}). This
invites reading $f_\theta$ as the right-hand side of an ordinary
differential equation $\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = g(u)$, in the sense of a
Neural ODE \citep{Chen2018}: a vector field that states how the state
changes at every moment. Under that reading, a model would define the state
at any time, its predictions for different intervals would be consistent
with one another, and $f_\theta$ could be interpreted as a rate of growth.
A network trained with one fixed discretisation need not have this
property. It can fit the observed data while its output depends on the
discretisation it was trained with \citep{Ott2021, Krishnapriyan2023}. The
solver-swap test determines which of the trained models have the property,
and therefore for which models statements about continuous-time behaviour
are admissible.

The test rests on a basic property of numerical integration. If a model
defines an ordinary differential equation
$\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = g(u)$, every convergent integration scheme
applied to $g$ approximates the same solution, with an error that shrinks
as the step $h$ shrinks, in proportion to $h^p$ for a scheme of order $p$
\citep{Hairer1993}. The same trained weights, integrated with a smaller
step or a scheme of higher order, must therefore approach one prediction.
Two criteria make this operational:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1, convergence:} when the step is refined, the change in
    the prediction becomes smaller;
  \item \textbf{C2, agreement:} all settings that are at least as fine as
    the one used in training in both step and order agree to within the
    run-to-run noise, the standard deviation of 0.0127 between repeated runs of one
    configuration (Appendix~\ref{app:full-results:criteria}).
\end{itemize}
A model is read as a continuous-time model only if it satisfies both.

The test requires no retraining. A trained checkpoint is rebuilt from its
own configuration and evaluated with the unmodified evaluation code, with
only the integration scheme or the step changed. The integration wrapper
has no parameters (\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}), so every setting uses the
same weights. As a control, the setting a model was trained with reproduces
exactly the change-region Dice recorded at the end of its training. Three
models were tested: the canonical dilated-stencil network, trained with one
Euler step and with $\Delta t$ as an input of the operator; the same
network trained inside the autonomous Runge--Kutta wrapper, with one RK4
step and without a $\Delta t$ input; and the T-FEN in its energy-conserving
form, trained with RK4 in steps of 45 days and without a $\Delta t$ input.
The two graph networks were tested on fold 2 and the T-FEN on fold 0, each
with one seed. The T-FEN was integrated with RK4 only, because an explicit
Euler step is unstable for its transport operator at any step
(\S\ref{sec:method:family:fen}).

Table~\ref{tab:experiments:swap} lists the results, and
Figure~\ref{fig:experiments:swap} shows them against the step size. The
canonical network fails both criteria. Refining its Euler step from one to two to four
sub-steps changes the Dice first by $-0.016$ and then by a further
$-0.060$: the change grows instead of shrinking (C1). Across the five
settings the Dice spans 0.086, almost seven times the run-to-run noise
(C2). The reason is structural. The operator receives $\Delta t$ as an
input, so a smaller step also changes the input on which the operator is
conditioned. There is no single vector field $g(u)$ to converge to: the
canonical network is a one-step map conditioned on the length of the step,
a single jump in the sense of \S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}.

The network trained inside the Runge--Kutta wrapper passes both criteria.
Its Euler refinements change the Dice by $+0.018$ and then by $+0.004$
(C1), and every setting except the single Euler step, the crudest one,
lies within 0.0044 of the others; the three RK4 settings agree to within
0.0005 (C2). The T-FEN passes as well. Refining its step from 45 days,
the training step, to 30, 22.5 and 15 days changes the Dice by at most
0.0003 (C1, C2). Only the coarser step of 90 days departs, and by no more
than 0.0015.

It follows that the canonical network, and with it every headline
result, may not be described as a continuous-time model: no statement about an ordinary
differential equation, a learned rate of growth or learned dynamics is
made for it. For the network trained inside the Runge--Kutta wrapper and
for the energy-conserving T-FEN, a continuous-time reading is admissible,
within the scope of one fold and one seed. The network trained inside the
Runge--Kutta wrapper is not more accurate than the canonical network
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:time}). The T-FEN lies slightly above it,
at the noise level (\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}), and owes this to
its transport term (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:transport}), not to
the continuous-time property. The continuous-time property can therefore be obtained on this task, but it
does not decide the accuracy.

\subsection{Numerical Stability of the T-FEN}
\label{sec:experiments:validity:tfen}

The T-FEN was first trained with the Galerkin form of its transport
operator (\S\ref{sec:method:family:fen}). Four of its five runs are
unstable in the sense defined there
(Table~\ref{tab:experiments:tfen-stability}), and so are most of the runs
repeated with the second seed, whereas the free-form FEN, which lacks only the transport term, is stable on every
fold. Tracing the rollouts step by step showed where the divergence comes
from. It starts at the edge of the crop, in a layer channel, driven by the
transport term, and only after about 1.6 to 2 years: later than the
one-year anchor and later than the 360 days that training unrolls. A
smaller step did not help, and the velocities at the onset were within the
stability limit of RK4, so the step size was not the cause. The cause was
the defect of the Galerkin operator at the domain edge described
in~\S\ref{sec:method:family:fen}. Because the divergence starts after the
anchor and away from the lesion, it did not affect the change-region Dice
of the Galerkin T-FEN at one year.

\subsection{Comparison with Mai et al. (2024)}
\label{sec:experiments:qualitative:mai}

\citet{Mai2024} predict GA growth on a larger MUW cohort from a single
baseline OCT volume, with a Neural ODE integrated by RK4. Besides Dice
scores, their evaluation uses two eye-level measures of growth speed,
which the Dice scores do not capture. Both are computed here following the
definitions of \citet{Mai2024}, as far as these are specified.

The growth rate of an eye is the change of the square root of its lesion
area per year, $(\sqrt{A_{\text{last}}} - \sqrt{A_{\text{base}}}) / T$,
where $T$ is the time from the baseline visit to the eye's last visit.
Taking the square root of the area removes the dependence of the growth
rate on the baseline lesion size \citep{Feuer2013}. The rate is computed
once from the ground truth and once from the model's rollout, over the
eye's whole follow-up as in \citet{Mai2024}, not at the one-year anchor.
Two measures compare the two rates over the cohort. The Pearson
correlation $r$ measures how closely the predicted rates follow a linear
relation with the true rates across eyes: $r = 1$ is a perfect linear
relation and $r = 0$ no linear relation. It does not require the two to
agree in scale. The fast-progressor area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (AUC) asks whether the predicted rate separates the
fastest-growing eyes, the top 10, 15 or 20\,\% of true growth rates, from
the rest: 0.5 is chance and 1 is perfect separation.

Both measures are computed for the canonical model. Each eye's predicted
rate is averaged over the late epochs, the 75 validation eyes of the five
folds are pooled, and 95\,\% intervals come from a bootstrap over eyes.
Table~\ref{tab:experiments:mai} lists the result.

\section{Computational Cost}
\label{sec:experiments:cost}

The cost of an arm is reported as its minimum epoch time over the five
folds. The mean is not used. The epoch times of a run carry a nearly
constant additive overhead of 17 to 64~s, which would penalise cheap
operators far more than expensive ones. The spread between folds has two
measured causes. The first is this overhead. The second is that the work
per epoch differs between folds: an epoch is twenty passes over the
fold's training eyes in batches of four, which gives 280, 320, 300, 300
and 320 optimiser steps for folds 0 to 4. Normalised by the number of
steps, the minimum epoch times of the five folds agree to about 3\,\%. All
runs used
one NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU and 30 epochs. The cost figures support
order-of-magnitude statements only.

On this scale, the U-Net is the cheapest spatial operator at 21~s per
epoch, about $0.23\times$ the 91~s of the canonical dilated-stencil graph
network. The FNO takes 23~s and the FNO with a $3 \times 3$ local path
29~s. The graph networks on the index-space graph take 76~s ($k = 12$)
and 108~s ($k = 20$), and the one-hop floor 37~s. The Finite Element
Networks are the most expensive single-step family: 180~s for the
free-form network and 367~s for the T-FEN
(Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms}).

Three ratios do not depend on the fine details of the timing. The
Runge--Kutta wrapper evaluates the operator four times per step. Over the
dilated-stencil network, where the forward pass of the operator dominates
the epoch, it raises the epoch time from 91 to 439~s, about $4.8\times$.
Over the FNO, whose forward pass is cheap, it raises it only from 23 to
45~s, about $2\times$, because the fixed part of the epoch weighs more.
The T-FEN costs about $4\times$ the dilated-stencil network. The
dual-branch moving-mesh extension costs about $10\times$ its
single-branch counterpart (Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde}).

Set against the results of this chapter, most of the extra cost bought
nothing measurable. The Runge--Kutta wrappers are nulls against their
single-step counterparts (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:time}), wider
operators did not improve on their original sizes
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:capacity}), and the dual branch is a
null (Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde}). The T-FEN is the one expensive arm with
an established effect, its transport term, but at the anchor it ties the
dilated-stencil network, which reaches the same level at about a quarter
of the cost. Inference time per rollout step and memory footprint were
not measured.
