\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol}

This section defines how the operators are scored and how two scores are
compared.

% Supervisor review 2, SV-5.1 (2026-10-18): heading was "Why Not Per-Pixel Error".
\subsection{Evaluation Challenges}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol:mse}

GA progresses slowly. Between two consecutive visits, almost all pixels of
the lesion mask stay unchanged. Persistence, the prediction that nothing
changes, is therefore a strong per-pixel baseline. A per-pixel mean squared
error or root mean squared error (RMSE) is dominated by the static pixels,
so a model can reach a low per-pixel error without predicting any
progression.

The same holds for a Dice score over the whole lesion. The Dice score
measures the overlap of two regions: it is 1 when they coincide and 0 when
they share no pixel. Over the whole lesion it is dominated by the static
interior, so persistence already reaches a full-mask Dice of about 0.87,
and the score barely responds to progression.

Raw errors are also not compared across architectures, because the
clinical metrics are computed on the thresholded mask. Under autoregressive
rollout, the dense operators, such as the U-Net and the Fourier Neural
Operator, spread their raw mask predictions much further from the two mask
values than the graph network does: the late-epoch mask RMSE of the rollout
is roughly twice as large for the U-Net as for the graph network, while its
thresholded metrics are higher. A raw-error comparison across architecture
classes would therefore measure this spread, not the prediction. In
addition, the soft-Dice term of the loss stretches the raw mask values away
from the threshold (\S\ref{sec:method:training:loss}). RMSE values are
computed and logged, but no RMSE value is used as evidence in this chapter.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol:metrics}

The headline metric is the change-region Dice at the one-year anchor: the
first visit of the rollout whose time since baseline reaches about one year
(\S\ref{sec:data:temporal}, \S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:rollout}).
% 2026-10-19 (SV-6.4, author): "anchor" kept; defined at its first use in
% Chapter 5 instead of renamed. In the version the supervisor read (2026-10-02)
% the term was used in 3.3 and 6.1 without a definition.
All predicted and ground-truth masks are binarised at the physical threshold of
0.5, which is mapped into normalised space in the same way as in the loss. Let $b$
be the thresholded mask at the baseline visit (the first visit of the
rollout), $p$ the thresholded prediction at the anchor and $y$ the
thresholded ground truth at the anchor. The predicted and the true change
regions are
\begin{equation}
  \hat{C} = \{\, i : p_i \neq b_i \,\}, \qquad
  C = \{\, i : y_i \neq b_i \,\},
  \label{eq:experiments:change-region}
\end{equation}
and the change-region Dice is $2\,|\hat{C} \cap C| \,/\, (|\hat{C}| + |C|)$.
Applied to the change regions, the Dice score measures how well the
predicted change overlaps the change that occurred; the large part of the
lesion that stays the same does not enter it.

Two consequences follow. First, persistence predicts no change, so its
score is 0 by construction, and every positive value is an improvement over
predicting no progression. Second, the metric is symmetric. A predicted
shrinkage counts as predicted change and is scored against the truth, which
is consistent with the absence of a monotonic-growth constraint
(\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}).

The change-region Dice differs from the growth-region Dice
of~\citet{Mai2024}, which this chapter also uses. The growth-region Dice
scores only new atrophy, the pixels that are lesion at the anchor but not
at baseline. The change-region Dice scores every changed pixel, growth and
shrinkage alike. Both score 0 for persistence and are easy to confuse:
Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms} and all differences between arms use the
change-region Dice, while the eye counts of
\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats} use the growth-region Dice.

Formally, the growth-region Dice compares the true new-atrophy region
$\{\, i : y_i = 1 \text{ and } b_i = 0 \,\}$ with its predicted
counterpart. It is used for the eye counts of
\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}. It is also computed at every
rollout step and grouped by the time since baseline into $(0, 1]$,
$(1, 2]$, $(2, 3]$ and more than three years. These horizon bins show how
the prediction degrades beyond the one-year anchor. \citet{Mai2024} report
the same metric in the same bins.

Because growth across the edge of the imaged field is censored
(\S\ref{sec:data:spatial}), the eyes are also split by whether their lesion
touches that edge; the split is reported
in~\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}. An eye counts as border-touching
when its true lesion at the anchor touches the edge of the imaged field.
This edge is the outermost ring of the crop or, for padded visits, the edge
of the zero padding, since for those visits the true edge of the field lies
inside the crop. Of the 75 eyes, 21 touch it.

Finally, a collapse counter records how many eyes have a thresholded
prediction at the anchor that is identical, pixel for pixel, to their
baseline mask. It detects a model that has learned to copy its input,
which would score exactly as persistence does. The eye-level growth-rate
measures used for the clinical comparison are defined
in~\S\ref{sec:experiments:qualitative:mai}.

\subsection{Rollout Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol:rollout}

All experiments
use the patient-level five-fold cross-validation of~\S\ref{sec:data:splits},
which has no test split; every result is a validation result.
Two evaluation procedures run after every epoch on the validation eyes of
the fold. The one-step evaluation predicts every validation window once
from its observed input. It isolates the accuracy of a single step from the
drift of a rollout. Every reported number comes from the rollout
evaluation. For each validation eye, it starts at the baseline
visit and predicts the next visit. The prediction is then fed back as the
input of the next window, and the rollout continues over the eye's real
visit schedule, with the actual $\Delta t$ of each window.

The one-year anchor is taken at the first step whose cumulative time
reaches it (\S\ref{sec:data:temporal}). For a few eyes this step lies
beyond one year, where the true change region is larger. This is accepted
because \citet{Mai2024} also score every follow-up visit by its time since
baseline and not at a fixed horizon. Persistence is computed inside every
rollout by the same code; for the anchor metrics it is the baseline mask
held constant. Per-step metrics are averaged per eye first and then over
the eyes, so every eye counts once, whatever its number of visits.

\subsection{Late-Epoch Mean}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol:late}

Each run is trained for 30 epochs and evaluated on the validation eyes of
its fold after every epoch. A comparison of operators needs one value per
run, and the way this value is formed affects every number in this
chapter. Every number reported in this chapter is a late-epoch mean: the
mean of the metric over epochs 10 to 29 of one run.

Single-epoch values are not reported for two reasons: noise and selection
bias. The validation score of a run is subject to noise. After an operator
has converged, its score still fluctuates between consecutive epochs
without a systematic trend, because each fold contains only 12 to 20
validation eyes (\S\ref{sec:data:splits}), so small weight updates change
the fold average noticeably. Over epochs 10 to 29, this fluctuation has a
standard deviation of 0.007 to 0.025 for the arms of
Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms}, the same order of magnitude as many of
the differences between arms. Choosing the epoch with the highest score
introduces, in addition, a selection bias. The maximum of thirty noisy
scores systematically exceeds the expected score of the operator, because
the selection favours epochs whose noise happens to be positive. For the
arms of Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms}, this bias amounts to 0.025 to
0.050, and it differs between arms: it is larger both for arms whose
scores fluctuate more and for arms whose score peaks early and then settles
slightly lower, such as the FNO.
% Changed 2026-10-18: "it is larger for arms whose scores fluctuate more" was
% only weakly supported (eleven arms of Table 5.1: Pearson r = 0.42 between
% the sd over epochs 10-29 and best minus late mean; FNO sd 0.008, bias
% +0.045).
It could only be removed by evaluating the selected epoch on independent eyes,
which a cross-validation without a test split (\S\ref{sec:data:splits})
does not provide.

In addition, the operators learn at different speeds
(Figure~\ref{fig:experiments:learning-speed}), and the mean begins only
once the slowest of them has reached its level. Most of them reach the
level they keep for the rest of training within the first few epochs, and
some, such as the FNO, peak shortly afterwards and settle slightly lower.
The free-form finite-element network and the U-Net rise more slowly and
reach their level only at epochs 8 and 9. A mean that started earlier
would include epochs in which these two operators are still learning,
which would lower their reported scores below the level they reach after
training. Over epochs 10 to 29, every arm has settled: the means over
epochs 10 to 19 and 20 to 29 differ by at most 0.014 for every arm.

The late-epoch mean therefore measures every operator after it has reached
its level and over the same epochs for every arm. Because no epoch is
chosen on the validation scores, it carries no selection bias, and
averaging twenty epochs reduces the noise of a single epoch. It does not
remove the variation between repeated training runs of the same
configuration; this variation sets the noise level against which
differences between arms are read (\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}).

\subsection{Noise Level and Comparisons}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}

Two sources of variation enter every comparison. The first is training
noise. Repeated runs of the same configuration do not give identical
results on the GPU (\S\ref{sec:method:implementation}): retraining the same
model on the same fold already changes its late-epoch score by up to about
0.02. The second is the fold. The five folds hold different eyes, and the
spread between them is larger than most differences between arms
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}). Two arms are therefore always
compared fold by fold, on the same five folds, and the five differences
are averaged.

% Supervisor review 2, SV-5.2 (2026-10-18): the comparison rule stated as a
% z-test with an empirically estimated measurement error. sigma_GNN from seven
% replicate pairs; sigma_UNet from the U-Net's two seeds on all five folds
% (NOTES, "U-Net run-to-run spread", 2026-10-18).
% TODO (author, 2026-10-18): re-read this z-test paragraph, the matching
% criteria in Appendix E.3 and the z values in the Appendix E tables when going
% through the thesis again.
A difference between two arms is tested against the variation caused by
retraining. Repeated runs of the same configuration on the same fold give a
standard deviation of $\sigma \approx 0.0127$ for the late-epoch
change-region Dice of one graph network
(Appendix~\ref{app:full-results:criteria}). If two arms did not differ, the
mean of their five fold-wise differences would vary with a standard
deviation of $\sigma_\Delta = \sqrt{2}\,\sigma / \sqrt{5} \approx 0.008$.
The ratio $z = \bar{\Delta} / \sigma_\Delta$ is compared with a standard
normal distribution: a difference with $|z| > 1.96$, that is, a mean
difference above about 0.016, is unlikely to come from retraining alone
(two-sided $p < 0.05$). The runs of the U-Net vary more: its two seeds on
the five folds give $\sigma \approx 0.019$, so comparisons with a U-Net are
read against a mean difference of about 0.020. A difference is called
established only when, in addition, it holds on most folds and most of the
75 validation eyes, does not depend on a single fold, and holds at the
second seed. Results are stated in these terms, for example ``higher by
0.064 on average ($z = 7.9$), on all five folds and on 70 of the 75
eyes''. The eye counts use the growth-region Dice at the
anchor, because the change-region Dice was logged only as a mean over the
eyes of each fold. In tables, ``mean $\pm$ sd'' is the mean and the
standard deviation over the five fold values, each the late-epoch mean of
one run.

The main comparisons were repeated with a second random seed, and the
conclusions drawn in this chapter hold at both. All numbers in this
chapter are for the first seed. Appendix~\ref{app:full-results:criteria}
gives the exact criteria, and Appendix~\ref{app:full-results:seeds} the
standard errors and test statistics of every comparison together with the
values at the second seed.

The per-pixel floor reads exactly 0.0000 on all five folds, the same as
persistence. On every validation eye, its thresholded prediction at the
anchor is identical to the baseline mask
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:metrics}). Every point of change-region
Dice in the table therefore comes from spatial context. The standard
deviations in the table are between-fold deviations and are much larger
than the noise level of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}. For the
$k$-nearest-neighbour graph network, the fold values range from about 0.43
to 0.52, and for every arm except the two FNO arms without a local path
the best fold is fold 2. Arms are therefore compared fold by fold; how the
folds differ is examined in~\S\ref{sec:experiments:folds}. Adjacent rows
mostly lie within the noise level of each other, so the order of the table
alone does not rank them.

The three highest arms are a finite-element network, a graph network and a
U-Net. The T-FEN reported here uses the energy-conserving form of its
transport operator (\S\ref{sec:method:family:fen}). Its first version, with
the Galerkin form, is not stable beyond the training horizon; the two forms
are compared in~\S\ref{sec:experiments:validity:tfen}. The T-FEN is higher
than the dilated-stencil graph network by 0.017 on average, on four of the
five folds. This lies just beyond the variation of retraining ($z = 2.1$ at
both seeds), but the lead is small, and at the first seed the T-FEN is
higher on only 46 of the 75 eyes, so it is not counted as established. The
dilated-stencil network is higher than the U-Net by 0.021 on average, but
on only three of the five folds. Because the scores of the U-Net vary more
between runs than those of the graph networks
(Appendix~\ref{app:full-results:criteria}), this lies just beyond the
variation of retraining at the first seed ($z = 2.1$) and within it at the
second ($z = 0.8$), and the two cannot be told apart. The T-FEN is higher than
the U-Net by 0.038 on average, on all five folds and on 51 of the 75 eyes,
and this lead is real.

\section{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients}
% Supervisor review 2, SV-5.4 (2026-10-19): heading was "The Ingredient
% Study"; the term "ingredient" is dropped thesis-wide in favour of
% "property" (2.3, 5.3, 6.1). Labels kept. Subsections follow goal ->
% results -> conclusion (SV-5.5).

The operators of Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms} differ in more than one
respect at once, so their scores alone do not show which property of an
operator matters. This section therefore compares operators that differ in
one property only, with the rest of the framework held fixed. Four
properties are tested, the ones named in~\S\ref{sec:background:neural-pde}:
whether the operator has spatial context at all
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:context}), how far that context
reaches physically (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:reach}), whether
global context needs a local path
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:global}), and whether an explicit
transport term helps (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:transport}).
Section~\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:convergence} brings the results
together. All comparisons are taken fold by fold
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}); one control trains on only four
folds (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:transport}).

\subsection{Spatial Context}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients:context}

The first question is whether an operator needs to see its neighbours at
all. It is tested on the graph network on the index-space
$k$-nearest-neighbour graph with zero, one and two message-passing rounds
(\S\ref{sec:method:family:floors}). With zero rounds, each pixel sees only
itself.

Without message passing, the network predicts no change on any eye and
scores 0.0000, the same as persistence. One round raises the score to
$0.4515 \pm 0.0243$, and a second round to $0.4623 \pm 0.0361$. The second
round adds 0.011 on average. This is within the noise level: the one-hop
floor is higher on one of the five folds and on 33 of the 75 eyes.

Spatial context is therefore necessary, and on this graph a single ring of
neighbours provides almost all of it. Along a row, one hop of the
index-space graph reaches only about 11\,\textmu{}m
(\S\ref{sec:method:graph}). The dilated stencil, tested next, was built to
remove this limit.

\subsection{Physical Reach at Lesion Scale}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients:reach}

The second question is whether the physical reach of the neighbourhood
matters. The graph network is kept with its loss, its data and its
67\,147 parameters, and only its edge set is changed. The index-space
$k$-nearest-neighbour graph, whose hop reaches about 11\,\textmu{}m along a
row, is replaced by the dilated stencil, whose hop reaches about
119\,\textmu{}m (\S\ref{sec:method:graph},
Figure~\ref{fig:method:stencils}). The dilated stencil is higher by 0.064
on average, on all five folds and on 70 of the 75 eyes. This result is the
reason the dilated stencil is the canonical configuration.

Table~\ref{tab:experiments:reach} varies the stencil around this default.
A reach of $\pm 12$ columns is lower by 0.019 and a reach of $\pm 42$
columns by 0.026, each higher than the default on at most one fold. The
$\pm 42$ geometry keeps the 20 neighbours of the default by doubling the
column spacing from 7 to 14, so reach and spacing change together. To
separate the two, the $\pm 35$ geometry keeps the default spacing and adds
neighbours (32 in total). It is also lower, by 0.010, within the noise
level, so a longer reach does not help even at the default spacing.
Widening the stencil across rows, to $\pm 2$ rows, lowers the score most of
all geometries, by 0.039. Figure~\ref{fig:experiments:reach} places these
geometries and the index-space graph on one axis of physical reach.

The number of neighbours was also varied at a fixed reach. With $k = 20$
neighbours, the index-space graph reaches as far as with $k = 12$. It is
lower by 0.010 on average, higher on only one of the five folds and on 20
of the 75 eyes. The fold average is within the noise level while the eyes
lean consistently one way, so the difference is not established; neither
view shows a gain from the extra neighbours.

The placement of the neighbours also accounts for the gap between the
graph network and the U-Net. On the index-space graph, the
parameter-matched U-Net is higher by 0.042 on average, on four of the five
folds and on 53 of the 75 eyes; fold 3 goes the other way ($-0.042$). On
the dilated stencil, the two cannot be told apart
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}).

Reach along the rows therefore decides how well the graph network does.
The best tested reach, $\pm 21$ columns (about 119\,\textmu{}m), lies close
to the 90th percentile of the per-visit front advance, 23 columns
(\S\ref{sec:method:graph}). Shorter and longer reaches score lower, and
more neighbours at the same reach add nothing. More reach through more
message-passing rounds does not help either: four rounds instead of two,
which double the reach of the stencil, lower the canonical network by
0.009 on average (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:capacity}). The gap to
the U-Net closed when only the placement of the neighbours changed, so it
lay in the graph.

\subsection{Global and Local Context Together}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients:global}

The third question is whether an operator with global context also needs
a local path. The Fourier Neural Operator sees the whole grid in every
layer through its Fourier modes but has no local path
(\S\ref{sec:method:family:dense}). This comparison pairs the plain FNO
with the same FNO plus a $3 \times 3$ local path, which adds 800
parameters.

The local path raises the FNO by 0.015 on average, on four of the five
folds and on 48 of the 75 eyes. The fold average is within the noise level
while the eyes lean consistently one way, so the effect is not
established.

Against the other operators, the plain FNO lies below the U-Net at the
one-year anchor: lower by 0.022 on average, higher on only one of the five
folds and on 30 of the 75 eyes. It is higher than the $k$-NN graph network
by 0.021 on average, but fold 4 carries all of it (without it, $-0.001$),
so this is not established. With the local path, the FNO cannot be told
apart from the U-Net ($-0.006$ on average). It is higher than the $k$-NN
graph network by 0.036 on average, on four of the five folds and on 49 of
the 75 eyes, and this does not depend on any single fold.

Beyond one year, the order between the FNO and the U-Net changes
(Figure~\ref{fig:experiments:horizon}a). In the growth-region Dice, the
FNO reads 0.546 against 0.563 for the U-Net in the second year, 0.583
against 0.581 in the third and 0.594 against 0.555 beyond three years.
These values are descriptive and were not tested.

\subsection{An Explicit Transport Term}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients:transport}

The fourth question is whether an explicit transport term helps. The
finite-element network computes the change of the state with a free-form
term, a small network evaluated on each triangle. The T-FEN adds a
transport term, which moves the state along a velocity predicted by a
second network (\S\ref{sec:method:family:fen}). The comparison pairs the
T-FEN with the same network without the transport term, at the same width
of 96. Both arms start at persistence, because their output heads are
zero-initialised (\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}), so the transport term is
the only difference.

The T-FEN is higher by 0.062 on average, on all five folds and on 68 of the
75 eyes. With the earlier Galerkin form of the transport operator, the
difference was of the same order, 0.053, again on all five folds. That form
becomes unstable in long free-running rollouts, but only well after the
one-year anchor (\S\ref{sec:experiments:validity:tfen}), so its score at
the anchor is valid, and the effect does not depend on the form of the
transport operator.

It has 34\,785 parameters against 68\,503 for the T-FEN, so part of the
gain could come from the extra parameters. A second control widens the
free-form network to a width of 139, giving 68\,282 parameters, level with
the T-FEN (\S\ref{sec:method:family:params}). This matched control does
not train on fold 4: its training error rises from the first epoch, and it
reads 0.047 at the anchor. A second attempt gave the same result, while
the width-96 control trains normally on this fold (0.501). With the second
seed, the matched control trains on all five folds, so the failure is
specific to this run (Appendix~\ref{app:full-results:seeds}). It is a
failure of the optimisation rather than overfitting, since the error rises
on the training data itself. Its cause was not diagnosed; the larger
T-FEN networks of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:capacity} fail in a
similar way, which suggests that the shared training settings suit the
larger finite-element networks less well.

On folds 0 to 3, the T-FEN is higher than the matched control by 0.072 on
average, on all four folds and on 59 of the 63 eyes. The extra width

\subsection{Summary of the Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:experiments:ingredients:convergence}
% Supervisor review 2, SV-5.10 (2026-10-19): heading was "Two Routes to One
% Deficit"; the closing "the architecture class is not what separates ...;
% the ingredients they carry are" replaced by the two measured magnitudes.

Two of the tested properties improve an operator against its own control,
and both concern how far an operator reaches in one step. The GA front
advances about 10 to 25 columns between two visits, with a median of 12
and a 90th percentile of 23 columns (\S\ref{sec:method:graph}). One hop on
the index-space graph reaches only $\pm 2$ columns, about 11\,\textmu{}m
along a row, so a single step of such an operator covers only a small part
of that advance.

Two different constructions remove this limit. The dilated stencil places
the neighbours of the graph network at lesion scale, so that one hop spans
21 columns (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:reach}). The transport term
of the T-FEN moves the state along a learned velocity
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:transport}). Each is established
against its own control, and the two arms lie within about 0.017 of each
other as the two highest arms of Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms}
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}). That two unrelated constructions
aimed at the same limit reach about the same score is the central
architectural result of the survey. It is consistent with the reach
explanation but does not prove the mechanism.

Spatial context is necessary (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:context}),
and a local path for the FNO is not established
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:global}). Within one architecture
class, changing one of these properties moves the score by 0.06 to 0.07;
between the three highest classes, the scores differ by at most 0.04. The
settings of the framework itself are tested
in~\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations}.

% matched control (5.3.4); three highest arms within 0.038 (Table 5.1).

from the global operators.

The first is the distance at which new atrophy appears. For every eye, the
share of its new atrophy at the anchor that lies more than 0.24\,mm from
the baseline lesion was computed; 0.24\,mm is about the reach of two
message-passing rounds of the dilated stencil
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:reach}). The larger this share, the
lower the dilated-stencil network scores relative to the mean of the T-FEN,
the U-Net and the FNO (Figure~\ref{fig:experiments:far-atrophy}; Spearman
$\rho = -0.32$ over the 75 eyes). On the third of the eyes with the least distant atrophy
it leads them by 0.035 on average, and on the third with the most it
trails them by 0.010. Fold~1 holds the most eyes of the second kind, with a
median share of 22\,\% against 5 to 16\,\% on the other folds, and its
three largest deficits of the graph network are eyes of this kind at both
seeds. Two of them are the two eyes of one patient. The pattern is
consistent with the reach argument
of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients:convergence}: an operator with
limited reach cannot place atrophy that appears beyond it.

The second property is contact with the edge of the window. The dense
operators fall behind the local ones most when the lesion touches the
window edge, by 0.079 on the 18 such eyes against 0.022 on the other 57
($\rho = -0.39$). Both dense operators see zeros beyond the edge, the U-Net
through the padding of its convolutions and the FNO through the zero
padding added before its Fourier transform, whereas a graph node at the
edge simply has fewer neighbours; whether this difference causes the
effect was not tested. The dense operators also gain where new atrophy
appears far from the lesion ($\rho = 0.29$), the mirror image of the graph
networks.

The dips on folds~4 and~3 have partial explanations. On fold~4 the graph
networks predict too little new atrophy. The fold holds the smallest
lesions, which grow most relative to their size: the new atrophy at the
anchor is a median 39\,\% of the baseline area, against 18\,\% (fold~3) to
35\,\% (fold~0) on the other folds. Every operator predicts too little when
growth is large relative to the lesion; on the third of all eyes where it
exceeds 40\,\%, each of them predicts less than half of the true new
atrophy. On fold~4, however, the dilated-stencil network falls shorter than
the FNO on the same eyes, with a median of 49\,\% of the true amount
against 83\,\%, and within the fold its deficit follows this extra
shortfall (Spearman $\rho = 0.79$, 12 eyes). Relative growth does not
explain the extra shortfall: across all eyes, the gap between the two
operators' predicted amounts does not depend on it. On fold~3 the new
atrophy lies closer to the existing lesion than on any other fold. This is
where the dense operators fall behind most, by 0.055 on the half of all
eyes with the least distant atrophy against 0.016 on the other half, and
within the fold they lose most on its two smallest lesions (0.11 and
0.20\,mm$^2$). Together these properties account for about a quarter of the
fold-3 gap.

The folds therefore differ mainly through the eyes they contain, and two
eye properties consistently separate local from global operators: new
atrophy far from the lesion favours the global ones, and lesions at the
window edge favour the local ones. These findings are exploratory. Several
properties were tested, two of the clearest cases are the eyes of one
patient, and the unexplained parts of the dips on folds~3 and~4 may come
from the training sets of these folds rather than from their validation
eyes; only retraining on a different split could separate the two. The
comparisons of the previous sections are taken fold by fold and are not
affected by the shift that all operators share.


\section{Negative Results}
\label{sec:experiments:ablations}

This section tests the settings that every operator shares, namely the
time handling of the update (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:time}) and
the training objective (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:loss}), and the
size of the operators (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:capacity}). Most of
these tests are negative results: comparisons whose difference stays
within the noise level of~\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:stats}. Such a
result is a null at the available precision only. It does not show that
the tested alternative is equivalent, and it is scoped to the operator,
the folds and the seed on which it was measured. Two parts of the
objective are the exception: removing them lowers the score clearly
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:loss}).

\subsection{Time Handling}
\label{sec:experiments:ablations:time}

The framework handles time in a simple way
(\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}). The operator makes one step over the
whole interval to the next visit, and $\Delta t$ enters twice: as an input
of the operator and as a multiplier of its output,
$u_{t+\Delta t} = u_t + \Delta t \cdot f_\theta(u_t, \Delta t, z)$.
Because every operator shares this update, two of its details were tested
on the canonical dilated-stencil network: the multiplication by
$\Delta t$, and the single step. The single step was also tested on the
FNO, a global operator.

Without the multiplication, the update becomes
$u_t + f_\theta(u_t, \Delta t, z)$, while $\Delta t$ remains an input of
the operator. The change-region Dice does not change: $-0.0006$ on
average, higher on two of the five folds and on 30 of the 75 eyes.
% Values: thesis_numbers.py pair ANISOGNN_final_dilmean_nodts
% ANISOGNN_final_dilmean, 2026-10-01 (arm 0.5252 +/- 0.0425 vs 0.5258).
% The k-NN run of this ablation is deliberately not reported (author).
The mask channel explains why. Its two values are a fixed distance apart
in normalised units, $-0.602$ and $+1.660$
(\S\ref{sec:data:normalization}). At a pixel that changes between two
visits, the update must add this distance whatever the interval. With the
multiplication, $f_\theta$ must therefore output the distance divided by
$\Delta t$, and the operator learns this division internally. The
multiplication changes how the same prediction is parameterised. It is
kept because the prediction then falls back to the current state as
$\Delta t$ approaches zero (under the assumption
of~\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}), and because it is the form MP-PDE uses
\citep{Brandstetter2022}. As a consequence, $f_\theta$ cannot be read as a
growth rate; whether the operator can be read as a continuous-time model
is tested in~\S\ref{sec:experiments:validity:swap}.

Replacing the single step by a classical fourth-order Runge--Kutta step
(\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}) evaluates the operator four times within the
interval and combines the results. In this setting the operator does not
receive $\Delta t$ as an input (the autonomous field
of~\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}); time enters only through the integrator.
Over the dilated-stencil network, the change-region Dice is 0.012 lower
than with the single step, on four of the five folds; over the FNO it is
0.006 higher. Both differences lie within the noise level. For the
dilated-stencil network, the Runge--Kutta step costs about 4.8 times the
training time of the single step (\S\ref{sec:experiments:cost}).

0.018 higher on folds 0 and 1 and the same on fold 2
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:validity:swap}).

\subsection{The Objective}
\label{sec:experiments:ablations:loss}

(\S\ref{sec:method:training:loss}): a mean squared error in which the mask

(Table~\ref{tab:experiments:objective}). The first uses a plain mean

second removes only the soft-Dice term. The third adds a monotonic-growth
penalty.

Two further arguments speak against the penalty. Under the pushforward
curriculum (\S\ref{sec:method:training:curriculum}), the previous state on
an unrolled step is the model's own earlier prediction. A pixel the model
wrongly marked as atrophic can then be corrected only by raising the
penalty. A direct computation with the training loss on one such pixel, at
weight 2.0, gives a penalty gradient 31.8 times that of the data terms, so
the total gradient points away from the ground truth. The penalty
therefore acts differently on steps that start from an observed state and
on unrolled steps. In addition, the reference segmentations themselves
shrink locally between most pairs of visits
(\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}), which a strict growth constraint would
contradict.

\section{Validity Diagnostics}
\label{sec:experiments:validity}

This section examines two properties of the trained operators that the
accuracy numbers do not show: whether an operator can be read as a
continuous-time model, and whether the T-FEN stays stable when it predicts
far beyond the training horizon. Two tests were designed for this purpose.
The solver-swap test re-evaluates trained models with smaller integration
steps (\S\ref{sec:experiments:validity:swap}), and a stability criterion
checks the free-running rollout (\S\ref{sec:experiments:validity:tfen}).
Their results decide which statements about the operators are licensed.

\subsection{The Solver-Swap Test}
\label{sec:experiments:validity:swap}

The update of the framework, $u_t + \Delta t \cdot f_\theta$, has the form
of one explicit Euler step (\S\ref{sec:method:dt-residual}). The
Runge--Kutta wrapper and the Finite Element Network integrate $f_\theta$
over several sub-steps instead (\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk},
\S\ref{sec:method:family:fen}). As discussed in~\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk},
both suggest reading $f_\theta$ as the right-hand side of an ordinary
differential equation $\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = g(u)$, in the sense of a
Neural ODE \citep{Chen2018}: a vector field that gives the change of the
state at every moment. Under that reading, a model would define the state
at any time, its predictions for different intervals would be consistent
with one another, and $f_\theta$ could be interpreted as a rate of growth.
A network trained with one fixed discretisation need not have this
property. It can fit the observed data while its output depends on the
discretisation it was trained with \citep{Ott2021, Krishnapriyan2023}. The
solver-swap test determines which of the trained models have the property,
and therefore for which models continuous-time statements may be made.

The test rests on a basic property of numerical integration. If a model
defines an ordinary differential equation
$\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = g(u)$, every convergent integration scheme
applied to $g$ approximates the same solution, with an error that shrinks
as the step $h$ shrinks, in proportion to $h^p$ for a scheme of order $p$
\citep{Hairer1993}. The same trained weights, integrated with a smaller
step or a scheme of higher order, must therefore approach one prediction.
Two criteria make this operational:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1, convergence:} when the step is refined, the change in
    the prediction becomes smaller;
  \item \textbf{C2, agreement:} all settings that are at least as fine as
    the one used in training in both step and order agree to within the
    run-to-run noise, the standard deviation of 0.0127 between repeated runs of one
    configuration (Appendix~\ref{app:full-results:criteria}).
\end{itemize}
A model is read as a continuous-time model only if it satisfies both. C1
is read at the two finest steps: from 22.8 to 11.4 days the prediction
changes by less than the run-to-run noise with both schemes, and the two
schemes agree to within it at 11.4 days.

The test requires no retraining. A trained checkpoint is rebuilt from its
own configuration and evaluated with the unmodified evaluation code; only
the integration scheme and the step change. The integration wrapper has no
parameters (\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}), so every setting uses the same
weights. As a control, each model was evaluated with the setting it was
trained with, which reproduces its recorded change-region Dice exactly. The
step is fixed in time: $h = 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16$ and $1/32$ year, that
is 365 to 11.4 days. Each visit interval is split into the smallest number
of equal sub-steps that are no longer than $h$, so no visit is skipped.
Both explicit Euler and classical RK4 are evaluated at every step. Because
the median interval between visits is 180 days, steps of one and of half a
year leave almost every interval in one piece. Three models were tested,
each on folds 0 to 2 with one seed:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the canonical dilated-stencil network, trained with one Euler step
    per interval and with $\Delta t$ as an input of the operator;
  \item the same network trained inside the autonomous Runge--Kutta
    wrapper, with one RK4 step per interval and without a $\Delta t$ input;
  \item the T-FEN in its energy-conserving form, trained with RK4 in steps
    of 45 days and without a $\Delta t$ input.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:experiments:swap} summarises the results per model and
fold, Table~\ref{tab:appendix:step-ladder} lists every setting, and
Figure~\ref{fig:experiments:swap} shows the change against the step for
the two graph networks. The
canonical network fails C2 on all three folds. Over the settings at least
as fine as its training setting, its Dice spans 0.054, 0.077 and 0.201 on
folds 0, 1 and 2, four to sixteen times the run-to-run noise. At the
finest step, Euler changes the Dice by $-0.037$, $+0.043$ and $-0.187$
against the trained setting. On folds 0 and 1 the two schemes approach a
common value at the finest steps (C1), but that value lies far from the
prediction of the trained setting; on fold 2 the prediction still changes
by about 0.03 per halving of the step (C1 fails). The operator receives
$\Delta t$ as an input, so a smaller step also changes the input on which
it is conditioned, and steps of 45 days or less are shorter than any
interval between two visits. The canonical network is a one-step map
conditioned on the length of the step, a jump in the sense
of~\S\ref{sec:method:family:rk}.

The network trained inside the Runge--Kutta wrapper converges on all three
folds (C1). From a step of a quarter year on, its RK4 predictions change by
less than 0.002, and its Euler predictions approach the same value, to
within 0.002 at the finest step. It therefore defines a single vector field.
C2 holds on fold 2 only. On folds 0 and 1, the converged value lies about
0.018 above the value of the trained setting, one RK4 step per interval. On
these folds, that step is too coarse to resolve the vector field to within
the run-to-run noise. The finer steps give the higher Dice: 0.545 instead
of 0.528 on fold 0 and 0.457 instead of 0.439 on fold 1, level with the
canonical network (0.540 and 0.453). On fold 2 the Dice does not change.
The network fails C2 because one RK4 step per interval integrates its
vector field less accurately than finer steps do.

\subsection{Numerical Stability of the T-FEN}
\label{sec:experiments:validity:tfen}

% Definition moved here from Section 4.3.6 (AI review 4b.31/4b.32, 2026-10-18).
% Source: SOLVER_FINAL_RUNS section 9.13.3 (criterion, fixed 2026-09-11),
% section 9.19 (diagnosis) and 9.19a (skew-form result, 2026-10-01).
Stability is judged on the free-running rollout of the evaluation: every
model predicts the next visit from its own previous prediction, over each
eye's whole follow-up of up to about six years, without any ground truth
fed back. A run is called stable if, in this rollout, the root mean
squared error of the mask channel and of the layer channels, both in
normalised units, stays at or below 5 on at least 19 of its 20 late epochs
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:protocol:late}). The normalised values of a correct
prediction lie within a few units of zero, so an error above 5 means that
the rollout has left the range of plausible states; in an unstable run it
keeps growing, up to values of several thousand. The criterion was fixed
before the energy-conserving form was trained.

The T-FEN was first trained with the Galerkin form of its transport
operator (\S\ref{sec:method:family:fen}). Four of its five runs are
unstable in the sense defined above
(Table~\ref{tab:experiments:tfen-stability}), and so are most of the runs
repeated with the second seed, whereas the free-form FEN, which lacks only the transport term, is stable on every
fold. Tracing the rollouts step by step showed where the divergence comes
from. It starts at the edge of the crop, in a layer channel, driven by the
transport term, and only after about 1.6 to 2 years: later than the
one-year anchor and later than the 360 days that training unrolls. A
smaller step left the divergence in place, and the velocities at the onset
were within the stability limit of RK4. The divergence therefore comes from
the defect of the Galerkin operator at the domain edge
(\S\ref{sec:method:family:fen}), not from the step size. Because the divergence starts after the
anchor and away from the lesion, it did not affect the change-region Dice
of the Galerkin T-FEN at one year.

$+0.009$, which is under the noise level. The gain lies on the lesions that

\section{Comparison with Prior Work}
\label{sec:experiments:qualitative}
\label{sec:experiments:qualitative:mai}
% 2026-10-19 (author): heading was "Qualitative Analysis and Clinical
% Comparison" with one subsection "Comparison with Mai et al. (2024)"; both
% labels kept here. The qualitative material lives in Figure 5.2, Section 5.4
% and Appendix D; the former TODO (per-eye rollouts, success and failure

No study reports results for the exact task and data of this thesis, but
several report comparable measures. The closest is the study
of~\citet{Mai2024}, who predict GA growth from a single baseline OCT
volume, with a Neural ODE integrated by RK4, on the MUW cohort from which
the data of this thesis are drawn. Three further studies report values on
related tasks (\S\ref{sec:background:related-work:spatial}). This section
compares the canonical model with them on the two kinds of measure they
use: the growth-region Dice by time since baseline, which scores where the
lesion grows, and eye-level measures of growth speed, which the Dice does
not capture.

The values give an orientation, not a head-to-head result. The study
of~\citet{Mai2024} differs from this thesis in several respects. It
evaluates 184 eyes of 100 patients with a mean follow-up of 32 months,
about 2.7 years; this thesis uses 75 eyes of 51 patients with a mean
follow-up of 3.3 years. Their model starts from the raw OCT volume, the
models here from segmented masks and layer depths. Their paper reports no
cropping, whereas the areas here are measured inside the
$49 \times 1024$ crop, which censors growth at its border
(\S\ref{sec:data:spatial}). The two studies also evaluate different sets of
follow-up visits. The measures below follow their definitions as far as
these are specified. One difference remains: their paper computes the
growth rate from the baseline and the respective follow-up area, whereas
here one rate per eye is computed from the baseline and the last visit. The
other studies differ further, as stated with each comparison.

\citet{Mai2024} report the growth-region Dice by time since baseline: the
Dice of the newly atrophic region, follow-up lesion minus baseline lesion,
averaged over the follow-up visits in each bin. The same quantity is
computed here for the dilated-stencil network, the U-Net and the T-FEN,
averaged over the follow-up visits of the rollouts and the late epochs
(Table~\ref{tab:experiments:mai-bins}). All three lie above the values
of~\citet{Mai2024} in every bin, by 0.18 to 0.29. \citet{Gigon2021}
predicted the progression of RORA from OCT en-face maps of layer
thicknesses and reflectivity, an input close to the one used here, and
report the same quantity in the same bins. Their values, 0.46 in the first
year rising to 0.72 after four years, lie close to those of the canonical
network, 0.50 rising to 0.66. \citet{Salvi2025} report a Dice of 0.70 on
their test set for the one-year region of growth, predicted from fundus
autofluorescence images. Their setting differs in two ways that favour a
higher value of this metric. Their best model receives two visits, at
baseline and after six months, and the lesions of their trial cohort are
larger, 2.54 to 17.78\,mm$^2$ at inclusion with a median of 9.03\,mm$^2$,
against a median of 6.7\,mm$^2$ here (\S\ref{sec:data:dataset}); the Dice of
a growth region rises with its size (\S\ref{sec:experiments:folds}). Their
region of growth is also measured from the six-month lesion over the
following year rather than from the baseline.

The growth rate of an eye is the change of the square root of its lesion
area per year, $(\sqrt{A_{\text{last}}} - \sqrt{A_{\text{base}}}) / T$,
where $T$ is the time from the baseline visit to the eye's last visit.
Taking the square root of the area removes the dependence of the growth
rate on the baseline lesion size \citep{Feuer2013}. The rate is computed
once from the ground truth and once from the model's rollout, over the
eye's whole follow-up as in~\citet{Mai2024}, not at the one-year anchor.
Two measures compare the two rates over the cohort. The Pearson
correlation $r$ measures how closely the predicted rates follow a linear
relation with the true rates across eyes: $r = 1$ is a perfect linear
relation and $r = 0$ no linear relation. It does not require the two to
agree in scale. The fast-progressor area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (AUC) measures how well the predicted rate separates
the fastest-growing eyes, the top 10, 15 or 20\,\% of true growth rates,
from the rest; 0.5 is chance and 1 is perfect separation.

Both measures are computed for the canonical model. Each eye's predicted
rate is averaged over the late epochs, the 75 validation eyes of the five
folds are pooled, and 95\,\% intervals come from a bootstrap over eyes.
Table~\ref{tab:experiments:mai} lists the result.

Two further studies report how well predicted and true growth rates agree.
\citet{Gigon2021} report a correlation of 0.52 over the whole follow-up.
The four OCT-based models of~\citet{Yoshida2025} reach a squared
correlation of 0.33 to 0.35 on 442 holdout eyes, an $r$ of about 0.57 to
0.59. Their models were trained on 1219 eyes to predict the growth rate
itself, defined as the annual slope of the lesion area in square
millimetres, fitted over all visits and measured on fundus
autofluorescence images with a larger field of view than the OCT scan.
The canonical model, with $r = 0.40$, lies below all three models on growth
speed.

The two kinds of measure point in different directions. On where the
lesion grows, the canonical model lies within the range of prior work:
above~\citet{Mai2024} on the same cohort, close to~\citet{Gigon2021}, whose
input is similar, and below the FAF model of~\citet{Salvi2025}, whose
setting differs in ways that favour this metric. The higher values
against~\citet{Mai2024} are not evidence that one approach predicts better.
The models of this thesis receive the segmented baseline lesion as input,
so its outline is given exactly, whereas the model of~\citet{Mai2024} first
has to find the lesion in the raw OCT volume. Together with the
differences listed above, this means that the comparison mainly shows how
much of the spatial forecast the segmented mask already carries. On how
fast the lesion grows, the canonical model lies below every comparable
model, with $r = 0.40$ against 0.52 to 0.61. Possible reasons are discussed
in~\S\ref{sec:discussion:clinical}.


\section{Computational Cost}
\label{sec:experiments:cost}

This section compares the training cost of the arms. Cost is reported as
the minimum epoch time over the five folds, not as the mean. The epoch
times of a run carry a nearly constant additive overhead of 17 to 64~s,
which would penalise cheap operators far more than expensive ones. The
epoch times also differ between folds because the work per epoch differs:
an epoch is twenty passes over the fold's training eyes in batches of four,
which gives 280, 320, 300, 300 and 320 optimiser steps for folds 0 to 4.
Normalised by the number of steps, the minimum epoch times of the five
folds agree to about 3\,\%. All runs used one NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU and 30
epochs. Inference time per rollout step and memory footprint were not
measured. The figures support order-of-magnitude statements only.

Within this precision, the U-Net is the cheapest spatial operator, at 21~s
per epoch, about $0.23\times$ the 91~s of the canonical dilated-stencil
graph network. The FNO takes 23~s and the FNO with a $3 \times 3$ local path
29~s. The graph networks on the index-space graph take 76~s ($k = 12$) and
108~s ($k = 20$), and the one-hop floor 37~s. The most expensive arms are
those that integrate their operator over several sub-steps: the free-form
FEN at 180~s and the T-FEN at 367~s, both with RK4 in 45-day steps, and the
dilated-stencil network inside the Runge--Kutta wrapper at 439~s
(Table~\ref{tab:experiments:arms}).

How much such an integration adds depends on how much of an epoch the
operator itself takes. The Runge--Kutta wrapper evaluates the operator four
times per step. Over the dilated-stencil network, whose forward pass
dominates the epoch, the wrapper raises the epoch time from 91 to 439~s,
about $4.8\times$. Over the FNO, whose forward pass is cheap, the epoch time
rises only from 23 to 45~s, about $2\times$, because the fixed part of the
epoch weighs more. The T-FEN costs about $4\times$ the dilated-stencil
network, and the dual-branch moving-mesh extension about $10\times$ its
single-branch counterpart (Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde}).

Set against the results of this chapter, most of the extra cost brings no
measurable gain. The Runge--Kutta wrappers are nulls against their
single-step counterparts (\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:time}), wider
operators did not improve on their original sizes
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations:capacity}), and the dual branch is a null
(Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde}). The T-FEN is the only expensive arm with an
established effect, its transport term. Its lead over the dilated-stencil
network at the anchor is not established
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}), however, and that network reaches
about the same level at about a quarter of the cost.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:experiments:summary}

This chapter answered the second part of the research question. Inside
the fixed framework, operators of three different classes reach similar
scores at the one-year anchor, and every point of change-region Dice comes
from spatial context (\S\ref{sec:experiments:main-results}). What
separates the operators is how far they reach in one step: placing the
neighbours of the graph network at lesion scale and adding an explicit
transport term each raise the score by 0.06 to 0.07 against their own
controls, and the two arms built in these ways are the two highest
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ingredients}). Of the framework's own settings,
the objective matters: it must emphasise the mask. The time handling, the
integration scheme and the size of the operators do not change the result
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:ablations}). The energy-conserving T-FEN can be
read as a continuous-time model, the canonical network cannot, and this
property does not decide the accuracy
(\S\ref{sec:experiments:validity}). Against prior work, the canonical
model forecasts where the lesion grows within the range of other models,
but agrees less well on how fast (\S\ref{sec:experiments:qualitative}).
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets these results.
