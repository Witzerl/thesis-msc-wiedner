\section{Clinical Motivation}
\label{sec:introduction:motivation}

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is the leading cause of irreversible
central vision loss in older adults in industrialised countries \citep{Boopathiraj2024}. Worldwide,
it ranks as the third most frequent cause of moderate-to-severe vision
impairment and the fourth most frequent cause of blindness
\citep{Flaxman2020}. The number of people with some form of AMD was projected
to reach 196~million in 2020 and 288~million by 2040
\citep{Wong2014}. Among these, the late non-neovascular stage --- termed
\emph{Geographic Atrophy} (GA) --- is an advanced, irreversible and
progressive atrophy of the macula that causes a marked loss of visual acuity
once the fovea is involved \citep{Vallino2024}. Approximately one million
people in the United States and five million worldwide are affected by GA
\citep{Singh2025}, with around 160{,}000 new diagnoses per year in the United
States alone \citep{Vallino2024}. Unlike
other eye diseases that cause vision loss, such as cataract, glaucoma or
diabetic retinopathy, AMD is not counted among the avoidable causes of vision
loss, defined as those that known, cost-effective means can prevent or treat
\citep{Flaxman2020}. Clinical care therefore focuses on \emph{managing} the
progression of the disease once it has appeared.

The disease burden is concentrated in the older population. Prevalence of GA
rises by roughly a factor of four per decade after age fifty
\citep{Singh2025}, and a South Korean national survey reports overall AMD
prevalence increasing from 3.6\,\% in the fifth decade of life to 34.0\,\%
at the age of eighty and above \citep{Song2025}. GA often affects both eyes:
bilateral GA was present in 39\,\% of the patients of one OCT cohort
\citep{Yehoshua2011}, and in the AREDS cohort the cumulative probability of
bilateral GA rose from 7\,\% at first GA development to 62\,\% at ten years
\citep{Boyer2017}. The two eyes of a patient also show a high degree of
concordance in the incidence, progression and area of GA
\citep{Boyer2017}. An ageing
population, frequent involvement of both eyes and eventual spread to the
fovea make GA a long-term disease that progresses differently in every
patient. The clinically relevant question is therefore not \emph{whether} a
patient will deteriorate, but \emph{how fast} and \emph{where} in the
macula the lesion will grow.

Until 2023, the therapeutic landscape for GA was empty: no treatment was
approved, and numerous phase 1--3 trials of GA and intermediate AMD,
addressing mechanisms such as neuroprotection, visual cycle modulation,
antiamyloid pathways and cell replacement, had failed during the preceding
decade \citep{Lad2023}. This changed in 2023 with the U.S. Food and Drug
Administration's approval of two complement-pathway inhibitors,
pegcetacoplan (anti-C3) and avacincaptad pegol (anti-C5)
\citep{Singh2025,Trincao2024}. In the phase 3 trials, both drugs reduced the
rate of GA enlargement by roughly 14\,\% to 27\,\% over twelve to
twenty-four months, but the lesions continued to grow and best-corrected
visual acuity did not differ significantly from sham at the trial endpoints
\citep{Lad2023}. The clinical implication is twofold. First,
treatment timing now matters: an intervention that only slows progression is
most valuable when administered before foveal involvement, because central
vision is largely preserved until the atrophy reaches the fovea
\citep{Boyer2017,Vallino2024}. Second, intravitreal injections carry both
patient burden and procedural risk, so identifying which eyes will progress
rapidly enough to justify therapy is the critical decision support task. As
\citet{Vallino2024} note in their conclusions, the integration of artificial
intelligence with structural OCT biomarkers holds significant promise, and
artificial intelligence applied to OCT has the potential to provide
predictive probabilities of GA development over time. This thesis pursues
that avenue.


\section{Aim of the Thesis}
\label{sec:introduction:problem}

Predicting GA progression from imaging is framed as a
\emph{spatiotemporal forecasting} task on the en-face projection of optical
coherence tomography (OCT) volumes. For each eye, longitudinal imaging yields
a sequence of macula-centred grids on which the lesion outline and the
underlying retinal layer geometry are traced; given the most recent observed
state and a target horizon $\Delta t$, the goal is to predict the full
spatial configuration of the lesion at the future visit. This differs from
earlier work on AMD, which predicts or characterises the conversion of an eye
to a later disease stage \citep{SchmidtErfurth2018, Vogl2021}. The output here is not a single risk
score but a complete map of the lesion at the next visit.


Such a forecast requires a model that maps the state at one visit to the
state at a later one. In this thesis, this map is called the operator.
Many architectures can serve as this model, among
them the neural solvers for partial differential equations on which this
thesis builds (Chapter~\ref{ch:background}). Which of them GA progression
requires cannot be decided by
testing a single architecture: if one model succeeds, the reason may be
its operator, its size, or the setup around it. This thesis therefore
separates the model from the setup around it. The setup --- how elapsed time enters the prediction,
how models are trained and how they are evaluated --- is fixed once, and
the operator is the only part that changes. Architectures from different
families can then be compared on equal terms. The closest forecasts of
this kind are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:background:related-work}.


\paragraph{Research question.}
\label{par:introduction:research-question}
This thesis addresses one question, divided into two parts:

\begin{quote}
  \itshape What is needed to forecast the progression of Geographic
  Atrophy from longitudinal OCT?
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item What kind of framework does the forecast need?
    \item What kind of operator does that framework need?
  \end{enumerate}
\end{quote}

The first part concerns everything that surrounds the model: how
irregular visit intervals enter the prediction, and how models are
trained and evaluated. The second concerns the model itself, the
operator that maps one visit to the next. Chapter~\ref{ch:method}
develops a framework that answers the first part and leaves the operator
as its only free component. Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} then answers the
second by comparing different operators within that framework.


\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:introduction:contributions}
% SV-1.6 literature check (2026-10-13): Delta t-conditioning alone is not

The main contributions of this thesis are the following.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A framework for irregular visit schedules that is independent of
    the architecture inside it: a $\Delta t$-scaled residual update, a
    pushforward curriculum measured in elapsed days, and a shared loss and
    evaluation. Operators from unrelated literatures are trained and
    evaluated in it under identical conditions, so that a difference in
    accuracy can be attributed to the model rather than to its training
    setup.
  \item A controlled comparison of several operator classes for
    forecasting GA from longitudinal OCT, at matched parameter counts and
    with five-fold cross-validation.
  \item The identification of physical reach at the scale of the lesion
    front and an explicit transport term as two independent routes to the
    same deficit: a single update that cannot see as far as the lesion
    front moves between two visits. This identifies the property a
    forecasting model for GA has to provide.
  \item Results on what did not help: larger models, a higher integration
    order, a monotonic-growth penalty and an extension with an adaptive
    moving mesh. These rule out directions that would otherwise appear
    promising.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:introduction:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} introduces GA and OCT imaging, the numerical
ideas that learned PDE solvers build on, and the families of learned models
considered in this work. Chapter~\ref{ch:data} describes the Medical
University of Vienna GA cohort and how each visit is turned into an
eleven-channel state. Chapter~\ref{ch:method} addresses the first part of
the research question by developing the framework, and describes the models
that fill its one free component. Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} addresses the
second part by comparing these models on equal terms.
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} discusses what the results mean clinically and
methodologically, together with their limitations, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes. Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde} reports an additional
experiment with an adaptive moving mesh.
