\section{Clinical Motivation}
\label{sec:introduction:motivation}

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is the leading cause of irreversible
central vision loss in older adults in industrialised countries \citep{Boopathiraj2024}. Worldwide,
it ranks as the third most frequent cause of moderate-to-severe vision
impairment and the fourth most frequent cause of blindness
\citep{Flaxman2020}. The number of people with some form of AMD was projected
to reach 196~million in 2020 and 288~million by 2040
\citep{Wong2014}. AMD progresses from early and intermediate stages to
two late forms: neovascular AMD, with abnormal blood vessel growth, and
macular atrophy \citep{Vogl2021}. The early stages are marked by drusen,
extracellular deposits of cellular debris \citep{Boyer2017}. The atrophic
late form without neovascularisation is termed \emph{Geographic Atrophy}
(GA). It is an advanced, irreversible and progressive atrophy of the
macula, the central region of the retina that provides sharp vision. GA
causes a marked loss of visual acuity, the sharpness of vision, once the
fovea, the centre of the
macula, is involved \citep{Vallino2024}. Approximately one million
people in the United States and five million worldwide are affected by GA
\citep{Singh2025}, with around 160{,}000 new diagnoses per year in the United
States alone \citep{Vallino2024}. Unlike cataract, glaucoma or diabetic
retinopathy, AMD is not counted among the avoidable causes of vision loss
\citep{Flaxman2020}. Avoidable causes are those that can be prevented or
treated by known, cost-effective means. Clinical care therefore focuses on \emph{managing} the
progression of the disease once it has appeared.

The disease burden is concentrated in the older population. Prevalence of GA
rises by roughly a factor of four per decade after age fifty
\citep{Singh2025}, and a South Korean national survey reports overall AMD
prevalence increasing from 3.6\,\% in the fifth decade of life to 34.0\,\%
at the age of eighty and above \citep{Song2025}. GA often affects both eyes:
bilateral GA was present in 39\,\% of the patients of one imaging cohort
\citep{Yehoshua2011}, and in the cohort of the Age-Related Eye Disease
Study (AREDS) the cumulative probability of
bilateral GA rose from 7\,\% at first GA development to 62\,\% at ten years
\citep{Boyer2017}. The two eyes of a patient also show a high degree of
concordance in the incidence, progression and area of GA
\citep{Boyer2017}. Progression is also highly variable between patients,
with the lesion area growing by 0.53 to 2.6\,mm$^2$ per year
\citep{Mai2024}. The clinically relevant question is therefore how fast
and where in the macula the lesion will grow.

Until 2023, no treatment for GA was approved \citep{Trincao2024}, and numerous phase 1--3 trials of GA and intermediate AMD,
addressing mechanisms such as neuroprotection, visual cycle modulation,
antiamyloid pathways and cell replacement, had failed during the preceding
decade \citep{Lad2023}. This changed in 2023, when the U.S. Food and Drug
Administration approved two complement inhibitors, pegcetacoplan and
avacincaptad pegol \citep{Singh2025,Trincao2024}. Both drugs block part of
the complement cascade, a component of the immune system. So far they are
approved in the United States but not in the European Union
\citep{EMA2024Syfovre,EMA2024Izelvay}.
In the phase 3 trials, both drugs reduced the rate of GA enlargement by
roughly 14\,\% to 27\,\% over twelve to twenty-four months. The lesions
nevertheless continued to grow. Best-corrected visual acuity, measured
with the best optical correction, did not differ significantly from a
sham (simulated) injection at the trial endpoints \citep{Lad2023}. Hence,
two things matter for clinical practice. First, treatment timing: an intervention that only slows progression is
most valuable when administered before foveal involvement, because central
vision is largely preserved until the atrophy reaches the fovea
\citep{Boyer2017,Vallino2024}. Second, both drugs are given as injections into the eye, which carry
patient burden and procedural risk. Identifying which eyes will progress
rapidly enough to justify therapy is therefore the critical
decision-support task.

Both decisions need a forecast of how fast, and where, the lesion of an
individual eye will grow. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) produces
three-dimensional images of the retina
(\S\ref{sec:background:ga-oct:oct}), and measurable features in these
images, called biomarkers, indicate the course of the disease. As
\citet{Vallino2024} note in their conclusions, combining artificial
intelligence with structural OCT biomarkers holds significant promise: it
could provide predictive probabilities of GA development over time. This
thesis pursues that avenue.

\section{Aim of the Thesis}
\label{sec:introduction:problem}

Predicting GA progression from imaging is framed as a
\emph{spatiotemporal forecasting} task on the en-face grid of the OCT
scan, the retina seen from the front. For each eye, longitudinal imaging
yields a sequence of macula-centred grids that carry the outline of the
lesion, annotated by graders, and the depths of the boundaries between the
retinal layers. Given the
most recent observed state and a target horizon $\Delta t$, the task is to
predict the extent and shape of the lesion at the later visit. Earlier
learning-based work on AMD predicts the conversion of an eye to a later
disease stage \citep{SchmidtErfurth2018, Vogl2021}; more recent models
forecast the GA lesion itself, among them that of~\citet{Mai2024} on the
cohort used here (Section~\ref{sec:background:related-work}). Like these,
this thesis forecasts a complete map of the lesion at the future visit.


Such a forecast is made by a learned update from the state at one visit to
the state at a later one. Its learned part, the change it adds per unit
time, is called the operator in this thesis.
Many architectures can serve as the operator, among them the neural
solvers for partial differential equations on which this thesis builds
(Chapter~\ref{ch:background}). Which architecture GA progression requires
cannot be decided by testing a single one: if it succeeds, the reason may
be the architecture, its size, or the setup around it. This thesis
therefore separates the operator from the setup around it, called the
framework. The framework fixes how elapsed time enters the prediction and
how the operators are trained and evaluated. It is fixed once, and only the
operator changes, so that architectures from different families can be
compared on equal terms. The closest forecasts of this kind are discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:background:related-work}.

\paragraph{Research question.}
\label{par:introduction:research-question}
This thesis addresses one question, divided into two parts:

\begin{quote}
  \itshape What is needed to forecast the progression of Geographic
  Atrophy from longitudinal OCT?
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item What kind of framework does the forecast need?
    \item What kind of operator does that framework need?
  \end{enumerate}
\end{quote}

The first part concerns the framework described above. The second
concerns the operator that maps one visit to the next. Chapter~\ref{ch:method}
develops a framework that answers the first part and leaves the operator
as its only free component. Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} then answers the
second by comparing different operators within that framework.


\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:introduction:contributions}
% SV-1.6 literature check (2026-10-13): Delta t-conditioning alone is not

% ImageFlowNet 2024), so the bullet claims the architecture-independent

The main contributions of this thesis are the following.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A framework for irregular visit schedules that is independent of
    the architecture inside it: a residual update conditioned on the
    interval $\Delta t$ between visits, a training curriculum that also
    feeds the model its own earlier predictions, with its depth measured in
    elapsed days, and one loss and evaluation for all models. Inside it,
    every operator with spatial context learned to predict change, and the
    loss decided whether any change was learned at all.
  \item A controlled comparison of operators from four architecture
    families (graph networks, a U-Net, Fourier Neural Operators and Finite
    Element Networks) for forecasting GA on 75 eyes, at matched parameter
    counts and with patient-level five-fold cross-validation. The highest
    operators of three families score within about 0.04 of each other at
    one year.
  \item The finding that, among operators with spatial context, the score
    depends mainly on how far one update reaches compared with how far the
    lesion front moves between two visits. Placing the neighbours of a
    graph network at lesion scale and adding a transport term, on a mesh
    with the same spacing, to a Finite Element Network each raise the score
    by about 0.06 against their own controls; this is consistent with, but
    does not prove, the reach explanation.
  \item Results on what did not help, each within the noise level: larger
    operators, a higher integration order and a monotonic-growth penalty.
    An extension with an adaptive moving mesh gave no measurable gain
    either; it is reported as one tested design
    (Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde}), not as a verdict on mesh adaptation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:introduction:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} introduces the anatomy of the eye, retinal
imaging and GA. It then covers the numerical methods for partial
differential equations (PDEs) and the neural solvers that learn parts of
them, reviews related work, and closes with the motivation for the
modelling approach and a formal statement of the problem.
Chapter~\ref{ch:data} describes the Medical University of Vienna GA cohort
and how each visit is turned into an eleven-channel state.
Chapter~\ref{ch:method} addresses the first part of the research question
by developing the framework, and describes the operators that fill its one
free component. Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} addresses the second part by
comparing these operators. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} discusses what the
results mean clinically and methodologically, together with their
limitations, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes. The appendices give
further details on the data, additional rollouts, the statistics of the
comparisons and the code; Appendix~\ref{app:mm-pde} reports an additional
experiment with an adaptive moving mesh.
